Everything You Need to Know About Rheumatoid Arthritis

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic autoimmune disease that primarily affects the joints. Unlike osteoarthritis, which stems from wear and tear, RA occurs when your immune system mistakenly attacks healthy joint tissue. This causes inflammation, pain, stiffness, and over time can lead to joint damage and loss of function. RA can occur at any age but often begins between ages 30 and 60, and it affects women two to three times more often than men.

Key Symptoms

Early recognition of rheumatoid arthritis is essential for effective treatment. Common symptoms include:

Joint pain and tenderness, especially in the hands, wrists, and feet

Morning stiffness lasting more than 30 minutes

Swollen, warm, or red joints

What Causes Rheumatoid Arthritis?

The exact cause of RA remains unknown, but several factors contribute:

Genetic predisposition: Certain human leukocyte antigen (HLA) genes increase risk.

Immune system dysfunction: B cells and T cells mistakenly attack synovial tissue lining the joints.

Environmental triggers: Smoking, air pollution, and certain infections have been linked to RA onset.

Hormonal influences: The higher prevalence in women suggests hormones may play a role.

Understanding these factors helps researchers develop targeted therapies to slow or halt disease progression.

How Rheumatoid Arthritis Is Diagnosed

Diagnosing RA involves combining clinical evaluation, laboratory tests, and imaging:

Clinical assessment

Review of symptoms, family history, and physical exam focusing on joint swelling, range of motion, and symmetry.

Blood tests

Rheumatoid factor (RF) and anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide (anti-CCP) antibodies: present in up to 80% of RA cases.

Treatment Options

RA management aims to reduce inflammation, relieve pain, prevent joint damage, and maintain function. A combination of medications and non-drug approaches is often recommended.

Medications

Disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs)– Methotrexate: first-line therapy; slows disease progression.– Sulfasalazine, leflunomide, hydroxychloroquine: alternative or add-on options.

Biologic agents– Tumor necrosis factor (TNF) inhibitors: adalimumab, etanercept, infliximab.– Interleukin-6 (IL-6) inhibitors, B-cell depleting agents (rituximab), T-cell costimulation blockers (abatacept).

Janus kinase (JAK) inhibitors– Tofacitinib, baricitinib: oral agents targeting inflammatory signaling pathways.

Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) and corticosteroids– Provide rapid symptom relief but are not disease-modifying.– Used for short-term flare control or bridging until DMARDs take effect.

Non-Drug Approaches

Physical and occupational therapy– Strengthening exercises, joint protection techniques, and mobility training.

Lifestyle modifications– Balanced diet rich in omega-3 fatty acids, regular low-impact exercise (swimming, cycling).– Smoking cessation, stress management, adequate sleep.

Assistive devices– Splints, braces, ergonomic tools to reduce joint strain.

RA is a lifelong condition that can fluctuate. Regular follow-up with your rheumatologist is crucial:

Assess disease activity and adjust medications as needed.

Monitor laboratory markers and imaging for silent progression.

Screen for medication side effects (liver, kidney, blood counts).

Vaccinations: keep up to date with flu, pneumonia, and shingles vaccines, as some therapies suppress immunity.

Potential Complications

If not adequately treated, RA can affect more than just the joints:

Joint deformities and disability

Osteoporosis due to chronic inflammation and steroid use

Cardiovascular disease: increased risk of heart attack and stroke

Lung involvement: interstitial lung disease

Eye inflammation: scleritis, uveitis

Increased susceptibility to infections

Early and aggressive treatment reduces the risk of these serious complications.

When to Seek Immediate Medical Attention

While RA itself is rarely life-threatening, serious infections, severe medication reactions, or sudden new symptoms warrant urgent evaluation. Contact your doctor or seek emergency care if you experience:

High fever, chills, or signs of infection (redness, swelling, warmth) around joints

Shortness of breath or chest pain

Sudden vision changes or severe eye pain

Unexplained bleeding or bruising

Living Well with Rheumatoid Arthritis

Many people with RA lead full, active lives. Key strategies include:

Education and self-management: learn about the disease, treatment options, and coping skills.

Building a healthcare team: rheumatologist, primary care physician, physical therapist, occupational therapist, nutritionist, and mental health professional.

Support networks: connect with patient support groups, online forums, or local arthritis foundations.

Goal setting: focus on realistic, measurable goals for pain control, mobility, and quality of life.

Final Thoughts

Rheumatoid arthritis is a complex, systemic condition that requires early recognition and comprehensive management. Advances in treatment have transformed the outlook for many patients, making remission or low disease activity an achievable goal.
