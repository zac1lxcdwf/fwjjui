# I Wanted to Push Myself to Build More Muscle. I Had to Learn to Fail the Right Ways for Success.

[Fail the](https://glycometrix.snaketongueofficial.com/)

I’M GUTTING THROUGH barbell curl reps, straining to finish the set. In the back of my mind, I know I had been set up to fail.

[the Right](https://glycometrix.snaketongueofficial.com/blog/2654406595.html)

Then again, that failure is the whole point. When it comes to building muscle, failure is often the first step to growth. That's especially true when you're training with a Maingaining mentality. What's maingaining? It's a unique approach to building the physique you've always wanted. The standard path to involves a typical bodybuilding bulk-cut cycle. But maingaining ditches all that overeating, pushing you to train just 100 to 200 calories above maintenance (which is a more sustainable long-term eating and training approach you can explore in greater depth in the rest of the new MH Ultimate Maingaining program).

To maximize so few extra calories for optimal muscle-building, you have to make every set count. And that means understanding failure. The pursuit of building muscle isn’t exactly a direct path up a mountain; it’s more like a series of small ascents as you rise and fall, gaining more ground with each attempt.

Failing—at least failing safely and effectively—isn’t merely pumping through reps until my body quits. The key, as MH fitness director Ebenezer Samuel, C.S.C.S. explains, is that there’s a right way to fail—multiple right ways, actually. To understand exactly how to push myself to my limits, Samuel is working me through a series of three moves to demonstrate the principles you need to follow for maximum gains.

SET 1:

Technical Failure

Exercise: Barbell Bench Press

[to Fail the Right](https://appetitecode.jcesqc8.com/)

The first principle, technical failure, is the simplest. “When you can’t do any more good form reps, the next rep would look like a completely different exercise,” Samuel says. Importantly, this doesn’t mean that I’m straining until I can’t lift anymore; this is dependent on keeping form sharp.

We demonstrate this with a heavy barbell bench press, which provides plenty of form checkpoints beyond just the binary lift or no lift. To do the exercise properly, I would need to keep my shoulder blades and butt on the bench, my feet planted and driving through the floor, and lower the weight all the way down to touch my chest. “If your hips are off the bench, you have broken form,” Samuel says. “Technical failure has a curve.”

I certainly feel that as I huffed and puffed between my final few reps on the bench, pushing the weight up off my chest and holding it aloft, mentally preparing myself for the next attempt. As I hit double digits, I knew that I could probably eke out just a bit more effort if I arched my back more and hiked my butt off the bench pad. But that would take the emphasis off the muscles I’m trying to train— so I push until Samuel had to lift the bar up from the bottom position when I couldn’t raise it myself.

[Wanted to Push Myself](https://beautybiometrics.hbsnzj.com/blog/1160152244.html)

Working with heavy weight also illustrates another critical concept: If you’re chasing true technical failure, do not go it alone. You need a spotter to fail safely. You should be giving your all up till the very last second—and when you truly fail, if someone isn’t there to grab the weight, the consequences can be catastrophic. “You have to push to that point, at least sometimes,” Samuel says. “My gold standard when it comes to failure is you don’t want to do it all the time.”

He suggests picking a few core lifts (for me, this would probably be the barbell bench press, back squat, deadlift, and push press) and working up to technical failure at least once every two months. “That’s going to tell you how it really feels to fail—and that’s critical for what we’re going to learn next.”

SET 2:

Two Reps in Reserve

Exercise: Barbell Biceps Curl

The second principle is more about working right up to the edge of failure, but requires that I understand my body enough to know when to throttle down. The concept is right there in the name; for two reps in reserve, I need to perform the given movement until I’d hit failure within two more reps. When I’m using this approach, I try to push right up to the point my brain starts to ask myself if I really want to keep moving. Working right up to this threshold of feeling is much more important than hitting an arbitrary rep count.

We use the barbell biceps curl to demonstrate this principle. After a warmup, the goal is to keep performing reps until I hit that 2 RIR ceiling. Samuel points out some signs to be aware of that would indicate I’ve reached my limit: “When the velocity of your reps starts slowing down, when it is markedly different between rep one and rep whatever number you’re at, that’s when you know you’re starting to fatigue.”

Good form is still paramount here too; if I were to cheat and swing my hips or break my posture to keep that barspeed up, I’d be failing in a different way—and defeating the entire purpose of the principle. Especially for this particular exercise, technique is the number one focus.

Even though I’m not pushing to absolute failure, this is still incredibly challenging. My biceps burn, and I fight against my instincts to shift my shoulders and elbows forward and rock my hips to shoot the weight up to the top. This type of struggle means I’m doing it right, according to Samuel. “When we think about 2 RIR, don’t think about [stopping] the moment it slows down,” he says. “Fight a little bit into that slowdown. Once you’re into that a little bit, that last rep should be a grind.”

I’m certainly straining, but the less intense nature of 2 RIR training means that it’s a tool I can use in my workouts much more often than technical failure—especially for isolation exercises like the barbell curl, which are less “aggressive,” in Samuel’s words, compared to heavy compound lifts. “On all your hard working sets, you want to think about living in that 2 RIR zone,” he says.

SET 3:

Advanced Technique Failure

Exercise: Seated Cable Row

Samuel explains that the final principle is a slightly more involved approach that requires an understanding of the first two components to be successful. We move over to the seated cable row machine to try it out.

Advanced technique failure requires something different than straight reps. The first part of the session is be a drop set, meaning I would perform a number of reps at one weight, then once I hit the point of fatigue (when the velocity of my movements slowed down, just like in the case of 2 RIR), we’d drop to a lower weight and continue performing more reps. This allows me to go beyond the failure point; even though I’m working with a lighter load, I’m still hitting those fatigued muscles in the same way. Machines are especially useful for this approach, since they’re easily and quickly adjustable and allow you to fail reps more safely than with free weights.

I work through a drop set once, and I absolutely hit a failure point. My rhomboids and lats are on fire with each pull, especially once we move to the lighter weight. As soon as I’m done, though, Samuel tells me to go again. Outwardly, I say sure. Inwardly, my back is ready to mutiny. I can tell I’m much closer to the failure point even on the first rep of the second round, so there would be fewer than in the first—but I know we’re piling on valuable volume.

“There is some diminishment in the reps he’s able to complete,” Samuel says. “The goal is to empty out as much as possible—you want nothing in the tank when you arrive to an advanced technique failure situation.”

The second set ends with technical failure, as I can’t pull the cable handle even halfway back to my chest by the final rep. I’m beat, and I just want to hunch over and give my back muscles a rest. They’re completely tapped—but I don’t feel like I’ve come up short. Not in the least. Now that we’ve pushed past my limits and totally broken down the binary of good or bad reps, I understand that in chasing my muscle-building goals, failure is my way forward to ultimate success.
