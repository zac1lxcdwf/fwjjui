But that pace slowed between 1939 and 2,000, where people gained about 2.5 to three months per generation. In fact, the researchers called this a “deceleration,” noting in the study that it’s unlikely to reverse course in the near future.

What’s going on here?

A LOT OF this likely has to do with what led to those big longevity gains in the first place, says José Carlos Andrade, lead study author and a researcher at the Max Planck Institute for Demographic Research. “In the past, big improvements came from reducing mortality at very young ages,” he says. Things like vaccines, the development of antibiotics, better hygiene, and clean water all likely contributed to this. “But since death rates at those ages are now already very low, there’s less room for further progress," Andrade says.

At the same time, he points out that improvements in health at older ages aren’t happening fast enough to make up the difference. Janet O'Mahony, MD, a Baltimore-area internal medicine doctor at Mercy Medical Center, agrees. “Once you make it to adulthood, you can worry about things like heart disease and cancer, which are the No. 1 and No. 2 killers in adults,” she says. “But because these illness affect older people, they have less of an effect on overall life expectancy.”

Keep in mind that the study looked at populations as a whole, meaning it didn’t parse out people who regularly suck down spirulina smoothies or spend time in ice baths. So, it’s hard to say whether certain longevity health hacks actually have an impact on lifespan based on this study alone.

Another complicating factor: You can also expect life expectancies to go down for a while before moving back up due to the COVID pandemic, according to Leonard Egede, MD, health researcher and chair of medicine at the University at Buffalo Jacobs School of Medicine and Biomedical Sciences. “Over 7 million people died from COVID-19 and another 675 million recovered,” he points out. “That level of loss of life will impact overall life expectancy in nations, and those who recovered may have impacts on their life expectancy later in life.”

ANDRADE APPLAUDS PEOPLE wanting to take better care of their health. “But some of the claims out there really stretch what the science supports,” he says. “There’s no hack that can add decades to your life.” Longevity is dictated by so many factors, including your genetics, lifestyle, healthcare, and environment, he says. “Healthy habits definitely help, but also real progress, from a population perspective, has always come from evidence-based advances like vaccines, better medicine, and public health,” Andrade says.

Something else to consider, says Egede: Many influencers are just trying to make a buck. “Because society has moved away from wanting data-driven information, influencers can claim something prolongs life with little evidence to back it up and it may not be questioned,” he says.

Andrade also stresses that people are making longevity gains. “But the pace of those gains has slowed compared to the past,” he says.

If you want to live a longer, healthier life, Egede just recommends sticking with evidence-based moves. “There are no easy solutions,” he says. “The things that promote longevity are likely the same as they have been for decades—regular visits to a healthcare provider for screening and vaccinations, maintaining a healthy diet, ensuring regular physical activity, and avoiding smoking.”
