How does alopecia affect mental health?

Reviewed by Yoshinori Abe, MD

Internal Medicine

Alopecia (hair loss) can significantly impact mental health, often lowering self-esteem and body image, fueling social withdrawal, and increasing the risk of anxiety (up to 39%) and depression (30%). Women and younger individuals tend to be most affected.

Key factors include identity shifts, the unpredictability of hair loss, and social stigma. Fortunately, effective supports exist: medical treatment, therapy, peer support groups, cosmetic options (wigs, scalp micropigmentation), and mind–body practices like mindfulness. Watch for red flags such as persistent sadness, hopelessness, or thoughts of self-harm—these warrant urgent professional help.

Explanation

Alopecia—whether patchy (alopecia areata), total scalp loss (alopecia totalis) or complete body hair loss (alopecia universalis)—can be more than a cosmetic concern. Hair often forms part of our identity, and losing it can trigger a range of emotional and psychological responses. In this article, we explore how alopecia affects mental health, why these reactions are common, and what steps you can take to protect your well-being.

Impact of Alopecia on Mental HealthResearch shows that hair loss can lead to significant emotional distress. In a review by Ongenae et al. (2005), patients with alopecia areata reported:

• Low self-esteem and negative body image• Feelings of embarrassment or shame in social situations• Worry about being judged or stared at by others• Social withdrawal to avoid uncomfortable interactions

More recently, Harris et al. (2018) conducted a multi-center study and found higher rates of depression and anxiety among people with alopecia areata compared to the general population. Key findings included:

• Up to 39% of patients showing clinical signs of anxiety• Around 30% experiencing symptoms of depression• Greater psychological impact in women and younger patients

Why Alopecia Triggers Emotional Distress

Identity and Self-Image• Hair often reflects style, youthfulness and cultural identity.• Sudden or patchy loss can feel like losing part of yourself.

Social and Professional Life• Concerns about appearance may lead to avoidance of work events or social gatherings.• Fear of negative reactions can heighten social anxiety.

Uncertainty and Lack of Control• Alopecia areata has an unpredictable course—hair can regrow, fall out again or remain unchanged.• This uncertainty can fuel ongoing stress and worry.

Stigma and Misunderstanding• Others may mistake alopecia for a contagious condition.• Misguided comments or questions can exacerbate feelings of isolation.

Common Emotional Reactions• Grief or sadness over the loss of hair• Anger or frustration at perceived lack of control• Anxiety about future hair loss or social stigma• Embarrassment leading to hiding or covering bald spots• Feelings of inadequacy or unattractiveness

Strategies to Protect Your Mental HealthWhile it's normal to experience distress, there are effective ways to cope and build resilience:

Seek Professional Support• Talk with a dermatologist or primary care doctor about treatments that may slow or reverse hair loss.• Consult a mental health professional (psychologist, counselor or psychiatrist) if you notice persistent anxiety or depression.

Build a Support Network• Join local or online support groups for people with alopecia. Sharing experiences can reduce feelings of isolation.• Invite close friends or family to learn about alopecia—educated loved ones can offer empathy and practical help.

Explore Cosmetic Options• Wigs, scarves, turbans or hats can restore confidence and allow you to express personal style.• If you prefer a natural look, consider scalp micropigmentation.

Practice Mind-Body Techniques• Mindfulness meditation, yoga or progressive muscle relaxation can reduce stress.• Regular physical activity boosts mood and self-esteem.

Challenge Negative Thoughts• Cognitive-behavioral techniques help you identify and reframe unhelpful beliefs ("I look odd without hair" vs. "My worth isn't tied to my hair").• Keep a journal of positive experiences and compliments to combat self-criticism.

Set Realistic Goals• Focus on small, manageable steps—trying a new hairstyle, attending one social event, or practicing a daily relaxation exercise.• Celebrate each achievement, no matter how minor it seems.

When to Seek Immediate HelpWhile alopecia itself isn't life-threatening, severe or prolonged emotional distress can be. Contact a healthcare professional if you experience:

• Thoughts of harming yourself or others• Inability to carry out daily activities due to anxiety or depression• Substance misuse as a way to cope

Remember, you don't have to face these challenges alone. Mental health professionals are trained to help you develop coping strategies and restore a sense of control.

Key Takeaways for Better Alopecia Mental Health• Hair loss can trigger real emotional and social challenges—recognizing these is the first step to relief.• Depression and anxiety are common; you're not "overreacting."• A combination of medical treatment, psychological support and self-care offers the best chance to maintain well-being.• Building a supportive community—online or in person—helps you feel understood.
