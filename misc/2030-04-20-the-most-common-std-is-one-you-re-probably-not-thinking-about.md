There’s a lot of stigma surrounding sexually transmitted infections. But they’re important to talk about, as more and more people — particularly men — are getting these treatable diseases.

Take chlamydia, the most common and prevalent STI in the country. More than 1.5 million cases were recorded in 2016, according to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. But many people still mistakenly believe that chlamydia results from risky sexual behavior alone, says Julie Dombrowski, MD, MPH, an associate professor and infectious disease specialist at the University of Washington. Instead, age, gender, sexual behavior, and sexual orientation all play a role in a person’s infection risk.

“For men who have sex with men, chlamydia most often affects the rectum or throat,” says Dombrowski, who is also the medical director of the Public Health–Seattle & King County STD Clinic. “We don’t know that much about how important chlamydia in the throat is [to long-term health]. We mainly find it because we're testing for gonorrhea in the throat, which is common, but we don’t really know the significance.”

In men, chlamydia typically causes symptoms such as penile discharge, pain, and irritation. These warning signs typically prompt men to visit an STD clinic or their primary care physician.

Men in higher-risk groups should receive at least an annual screening.

For women, though, it’s a different story. Chlamydia is an asymptomatic, “silent infection,” so women can unwittingly pass it on to a partner. If left untreated, chlamydia can have severe, long-term consequences for women, including pelvic inflammatory disease and infertility. Which is why STI screening is essential, regardless of gender or sexual orientation.

There’s no recommended screening frequency for men because “with men, we know they have chlamydia because they have symptoms,” Dombrowski says.

In Seattle, where she is based, local health department guidelines suggest methamphetamine users, men who have 10 or more sexual partners a year, and men who have partners who are STD positive get screened every three months. Local recommendations may differ in your area.

If you test positive for chlamydia, don't stress. “There’s an easy simple treatment: [the antibiotic] Azithromycin, 1 gram once by mouth, the end,” Dombrowski says. Another treatment option is doxycycline, which requires one pill twice a day for a week. Depending on where you live, you may be able to get a dose of prescription medication for your partner — without your partner needing to visit a doctor. It’s an approach called patient-delivered partner therapy.
