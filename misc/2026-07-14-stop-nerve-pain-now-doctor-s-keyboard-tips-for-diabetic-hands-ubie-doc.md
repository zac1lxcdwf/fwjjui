Understanding Diabetic Neuropathy in the Hands

Diabetic neuropathy is nerve damage resulting from chronically elevated blood sugar levels. In the hands, it may present as:

Numbness or decreased sensation

Low-force or mechanical switches require less pressure.

Keyboard Positioning

Keep your forearms parallel to the floor or slightly declined.

Position the keyboard directly in front of you, about elbow-distance away.

Lifestyle and Medical Approaches

Addressing underlying diabetes management and nerve health can reduce pain:

Blood Sugar Control

Consistent glucose targets slow neuropathy progression.

Work with your diabetes care team to adjust medications or insulin.

Nutritional Support

A balanced diet rich in leafy greens, whole grains, and lean protein.

Supplements (under doctor supervision): B-complex vitamins, alpha-lipoic acid.

Red Light Therapy for Neuropathy in Hands and Typing

Red light therapy—also known as low-level laser therapy (LLLT) or photobiomodulation—uses near-infrared wavelengths to stimulate cell repair and reduce inflammation.

How It Works

Photons penetrate the skin, energizing mitochondria in nerve and muscle cells.

Increased ATP production supports tissue repair and healthy nerve function.

Reduced pro-inflammatory cytokines may ease neuropathic pain.

Evidence and Benefits

Small clinical studies report decreased pain scores and improved sensation in diabetic neuropathy.

Users often note reduced tingling and enhanced comfort during fine motor tasks like typing.

Sessions are generally painless, noninvasive, and can be done at home with FDA-cleared devices.

Incorporating into Your Routine

Session Frequency: 2–3 times per week, 10–20 minutes per session.

Target Areas: Focus on the palms, fingers, and wrists where symptoms are most pronounced.

Device Selection: Choose units emitting 630–850 nm wavelengths with proven safety profiles.

Safety Tips: Wear protective goggles if recommended, and follow manufacturer guidelines.

While many find red light therapy helpful, results vary. It's best used alongside other treatments rather than as a solo solution.

Exercises and Hand Stretches

Regular movement promotes circulation, reduces stiffness, and helps maintain dexterity:

Finger Extensions

Place a rubber band around your fingers and thumb.

Spread fingers outward against the band's resistance.

Hold 5 seconds; repeat 10–15 times.

Wrist Flexor Stretch

Extend one arm forward, palm up.

Gently pull back on the fingers with opposite hand.

Hold 20–30 seconds; switch sides.

Wrist Extensor Stretch

Extend arm forward, palm down.

Pull fingers down toward floor with other hand.

Hold 20–30 seconds; alternate.

Tendon Glides

Start with fingers straight.

Bend the top and middle joints while keeping the wrist straight.

Return and repeat 10 times.

Perform these 2–3 times daily, especially before and after typing sessions.

Tips for Daily Typing Comfort

Take microbreaks every 20–30 minutes: release your hands, shake them out, or change posture.

Use speech-to-text software for long documents to reduce total typing time.

Wear compression gloves designed for neuropathy: light pressure can improve proprioception.

Alternate tasks: switch between typing, reading, and other activities to vary hand movements.

Recognizing When to Seek Professional Help

If you experience any of the following, reach out to your healthcare provider promptly:

Sudden increase in hand weakness or inability to hold objects

Signs of infection (redness, warmth, swelling) in your hands

Blood sugar levels consistently above target despite adjustments

New symptoms such as severe burning pain that disrupts sleep or daily life

Partnering with Your Doctor

Managing diabetic neuropathy is a team effort. Discuss:

Adjustments to your diabetes treatment plan

Prescription pain or nerve-modulating medications

Referral to a physical or occupational therapist for custom splints or exercises

Suitability of red light therapy and any potential interactions

Always speak to a doctor about anything that could be life-threatening or serious. Early intervention and a personalized approach can preserve your hand function and keep you typing comfortably for years to come.
