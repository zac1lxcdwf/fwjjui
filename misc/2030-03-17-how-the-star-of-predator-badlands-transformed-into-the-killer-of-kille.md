# How the Star of ‘Predator: Badlands’ Transformed Into the “Killer of Killers”

DIMITRIUS SCHUSTER-KOLOAMATANGI had no idea what he was walking into when he first auditioned for Predator: Badlands. The casting call had described "a creature," but he couldn't have imagined he was trying out to play one of the trophy-hunting aliens who rightfully hold the reputation of science-fiction’s deadliest species—or that, upon winning the role of the franchise's first-ever Predator protagonist, the film would test every component of his physical and mental endurance.

Born and raised in New Zealand, Schuster-Koloamatangi is of Samoan and Tongan heritage, and he improvised an alien language for his audition by bringing the two dialects together. "I was freestyling, really," he recalls. It was a strong audition, but filmmaker Dan Trachtenberg (Prey) also needed to see what the actor was capable of physically, so he set up an "American Ninja Warrior-style" obstacle course with the help of stunt performers. "The way Dimitrius navigated that course was insane," Trachtenberg says. "The swashbuckle he brought to all the sliding, jumping, leaping, and weapon work was even cooler than the stuntmen. Dimitrius had a special physicality."

But that initial test was nothing compared to what awaited the actor on set in his native New Zealand. While navigating dense forests, black sand beaches, limestone canyons, and rushing rivers, Schuster-Koloamatangi wore a 33-pound suit to play the role of Dek, a young Predator outcast who travels the deadliest planet in the galaxy on a journey for the ultimate adversary. It wasn't the only weight he carried—co-star Elle Fanning, playing a damaged synthetic robot Dek encounters on his mission, spent most of filming strapped to his back.

The challenge was intensely physical in ways both big and small—including training sessions over Zoom with linguist Britton Watkins, who created the alien language of Yautja specifically for the film. Schuster-Koloamatangi would have mandibles and fangs added to his face digitally during post-production, but his face would be uncovered during filming to allow for better capture of his facial expressions. That meant that he'd have to speak the language without engaging the parts of his face that wouldn't appear as part of the creature's finale physiology. (For instance: No lips, so no sounds that you’d need lips to make.) That meant Schuster-Koloamatangi had to train his vocal chords, adjust his body posture, and use unfamiliar muscles in his throat to fully produce all the right syllables.

All the attention to detail paid off. Predator: Badlands opened at the top of the box office and as the most successful movie in the Predator franchise, with fans and critics giving it raves. Schuster-Koloamatangi gave Men's Health a behind-the-scenes look at all the prep that went into the hit sci-fi sequel. He recounts the fight training, the workout regimen, and the mental health routines that got him in shape to play a killer of killers.

MEN’S HEALTH: Tell me about the process of auditioning for a role like Dek in Predator: Badlands.

DIMITRIUS SCHUSTER-KOLOAMATANGI: When I first got the audition for Predator, I didn't know what it was for. I didn't know what franchise it was for or what character I was playing. It literally just said that I was playing "a creature" and to do one take in English, and one take in my own, made-up language.

At the callback, I had to do an obstacle course. I had never done anything like it—and at that point I still wasn’t sure what the franchise was. They were drip-feeding us information, little by little. They told us the studio was Fox, and that the director was Dan Trachtenberg, so I figured it was Predator, but I still didn’t know for sure.

Then I met with Dan and he told me what the job was. This is such an iconic character, and Dan had a very unique way of approaching both the character and this story, which is the first in the franchise where the Predator is the protagonist. Initially, I didn’t know how that would work, but the way Dan articulated the story, the way we follow Dek on his first hunt, illustrated the world in such a cool way. Then he sent me the script, and it wasn't until after reading it that I realized just how amazing the project was.

MH: You worked with stunt coordinator Jacob Tomuri to tailor Dek’s fighting style around agility rather than brute size, given that he’s the runt of his clan. What added challenge did that pose for you?

DSK: That was a tricky balance. Because Dek is an undersized Yautja, he’s different to any kind of Predator we've seen in the films before. He moves differently, and he has a bit more agility. At the same time, we had to keep some of that heaviness—because even though he's undersized for his kind, he's still a big boy in comparison to a human. The Yautja are heavy by nature. Their whole biology is different. I had to keep the same animalistic, primal, physical traits that all Predators have while also finding the unique advantages that come with Dek's size.

I haven't done anything as stunt-heavy or as physical as this before. I had to jump out of a window once, and I’d done a little fight scene that only shot for a day. But that's it. I thought I always came prepared until I had to do pre-production for this movie—then we got into vigorous training, and I thought, Oh, this is going to be a journey. This is going to be something big.

MH: You also trained with swords that were deliberately heavier than the ones you’d use on set. How did that factor into your fight choreography?

DSK: I always loved action movies growing up. As a bored kid in New Zealand, I would go outside to find sticks in the backyard or at the park and play around pretending they were spears for literally hours. The general coordination of wielding a sword, doing the flicks and tricks, came naturally. But obviously it needed polishing and choreography.

During fight training, Vincent Bouillon, our fight coordinator, essentially stuck two swords together into one sword and said, "If you use this in pre-production, it'll just make it so much easier when you're on set. You won't even think about it. It'll be second nature, it'll feel so light. You can just breeze through it." And I just said, "Yeah, that's cool!" But that was before I actually picked up the double-sword. Then when we actually started training, my forearms were dead. My wrists were sore. It was tough. But using that double-sword gave me a lot of grip strength that I didn't know I needed. Vincent was right: When we got onto set, it was so much easier, and the choreography felt like second nature.

MH: Take me inside your training regimen. What did you focus on most?

DSK: Initially, it was just about movement. The first two weeks were about finding Dek’s physicality. I had to go do my research and build the character I wanted, then I would bring it to the stunt team. Once we found his physicality, the next step was molding it into the way he would fight. From there, we started introducing the swords, the spears, and all the different beats we would hit with those. Then we could start building the choreography for each scene. And before every session, we would warm up the body, stretch it out, go into stunt rolls and stunt falls. That was our stunt training.

But I would do my own training too. I knew which parts of my body needed the most strengthening. Because of all the sword work, I needed to strengthen my shoulders, my biceps, my forearms, and especially my wrists. So there was a lot of strength training and body-weight training at the gym to ensure that I had the endurance to get through those days. There were also a lot of leg days, just so I could withstand being in the suit for long stretches.

MH: This film shows us much more Yautja culture than we’ve seen in the previous Predator films. Your character is the runt in a clan that won’t abide weakness. There's honor and ritual to that, but it’s also brutal. What helped you get into the headspace of Dek, to find his place relative to this violent, masculine culture?

DSK: It was about immersing myself in anything Predator—the previous movies and the Dark Horse Comics were especially important to meticulously understand Yautja culture. Understanding Dek meant understanding what it means to grow up Yautja. People see it, like you said, as brutal or violent. But this is just their way of life. This is an average Tuesday for them. Their whole society is built on hunting; that’s how they gain honor. It's how they gain status within their communities. They can't live properly within their society if they are not elite hunters.

The whole reason they even hunt the way that they do is because they used to be oppressed, and the way they came out of oppression was through hunting. The first trophy that was taken was of the oppressors. They take trophies in honor of their ancestors. It’s not something they just do because it's brutal. That's something a lot of people confuse about Yautja when they see them in previous Predator movies: They just see them as these barbaric aliens who come in, kill everything, and cause havoc. But there's a reason they're doing it. There's a lot of honor in it for them, and it's part of a code they follow.

In this instance, he's trying to live up to this symbol of what it takes to be a Yautja. Their custom is that you kill off your weak. That's just how it is. If you're not pulling your weight, if you can't elevate yourself to where you need to be, then you have no value within their societal system. Because he's the size he is as a Yautja, Dek has to try even harder to earn his stripes, and he's outcast because of it. He's seen as a weakling. He's trying to live up to their standards, but at the same time he's trying to find—within himself—who he is. He may not fit into this one system, but that doesn't mean that he has no value, full stop. Through Dek's rage and the redemption arc in the film, we learn the Yautja have become more nuanced. He finds himself and his own clan.

MH: There's something about discipline in that as well—proving yourself to yourself, rather than to an external power.

DSK: He's no longer trying to prove himself to his clan. He's proving himself to himself. He becomes the Dek he needs to be to survive, because if he'd stayed the same, then he wouldn't survive on Yautja Prime.

MH: Having Elle Fanning strapped to your back must have been an incredible core exercise. What did that do for your physical coordination and endurance, and what was it like to bond with your co-star in such an unconventional way?

[Star of Predator Badlands](https://tendonforge.dns0663.com/blog/7158612748.html)

DSK: It did come with a lot of core exercise. [Laughs] And in terms of our dynamic and getting on as co-stars, it was really unique. I had never been in a situation where you had to be in such close proximity to your co-star for such long periods of time—and when I say "close proximity," I mean that we were physically touching the whole time. It fostered a lot of trust between us, which I feel helped not only with our chemistry, but also helped us be able to support each other. I felt really supported and—I'm only speaking on this because Elle herself has told me this—she felt really supported too. When you're constantly strapped to each other, you have to communicate. If something is making you uncomfortable, you speak up. If something is hurting you, you have to be very vocal—especially because we were both wearing harnesses. Elle was in a harness, but her legs also had to be rigged up, so she wasn't in a comfortable position. She was facing discomfort the whole time we were shooting. And we couldn't afford to be dragging around Elle Fanning, with her in despair and her feeling she couldn't speak up! So it was important that both of us were in a space where we felt comfortable with each other and felt that we were getting through it together. It created a very unique bond between us, one that I'm very grateful that we had. She's an amazing actress, man.

MH: Elle noted in a few interviews that you brought such positivity to the set—and she could always hear you coming, because you carried a boombox around. Was that all Yautja sounds for rehearsing dialogue, or does music play a role in helping you maintain your mindset on set?

DSK: Once I got on set, I needed music to help calm my mind. Especially as the shoot went on, it could have been easy to crumble under the weight of it all. My body was constantly sore. I was constantly hot. It was constant discomfort, essentially, being in the suit. Music was just the way I could stay positive and take my mind off what my body was feeling. It was a way to step outside myself and remain calm, to stay upbeat and stay energized. As a Yautja, you carry yourself with a certain physicality. There's a way you approach things. I couldn't walk onto the set without that energy, and music did help me get into that mindset.

MH: Were any particular artists or genres in heavy rotation?

DSK: It's funny, because at the start of the day, it would be real intense rap. I listened to a lot of drill and rap. It's very dark and violent, but that played into my character, the energy that I was trying to bring to Dek. And then, by the end of the day, it would turn into reggae and more mellow stuff, just to unwind and settle back into Dimitrius, so that we could do it all again the next day. There was a wide range of music going on throughout the set—a whole DJ disaster, as I like to call it.

MH: I love you giving yourself that emotional arc through music. You need to be able to get into an intense zone and stay there, but then you've got to come back into yourself at the end of the day.

You're always tense. You're always looking out. You're an apex predator. You carry yourself differently—and carrying that mindset for a long period of time, it's too much. I used music to pull myself out of that, to understand that this is a character and to bring myself out of those tense moments.
