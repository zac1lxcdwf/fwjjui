# This Man Lost His Hand in an Accident. Then Bodybuilding Turned His Life Around

After losing his hand in an accident, Diego Hernandez's life consisted of surgeries and bouts of depression — until he turned 21 and discovered bodybuilding, that is.

“The moment it happened, I don’t remember anything,” Hernandez tells MensHealth.com of the electrical burn that led to the loss of his left hand and nearly all use of his right at just 10 years old. “But then, when I woke up in the hospital it was pretty painful.”

Even as a kid, Hernandez knew the gym would help him in his recovery — insisting to his psychologists that he was going to ride a bike somehow.

“But when I was 21 years old, I got introduced to people who were serious about bodybuilding,” he recalls. And now, at the age of 25, he’s training to compete in his very first competition.

To get in shape for competition, Hernandez sought the help of a trainer in New York to hone his routine and eating habits. But because there aren’t a lot of resources out there for adaptive athletes, Hernandez said a lot of his moves are simply made up along the way.

“I started researching a lot on YouTube and Instagram,” he explained, often finding other adaptive bodybuilders who shared how they got the moves done as inspiration.

Hernandez is currently working out five days a week with his trainer. His moves, he explained, are simply variations on the classics. “There were certain movements I couldn’t do back in the day, but now, as days go by I find ways to learn how to do them myself in ways that work for me,” he said.

Hernandez said his week always starts on Mondays with leg day. He’ll perform a circuit usually performing each move with four sets of 15 that includes leg extensions, squats, deadlifts and leg presses. He’ll do everything in a slow, controlled movement so he can make a “muscle and mind connection so I know I’m working.”

On a different day, he’ll work on arms. Pull movements are hardest for him, but push movements like bench presses feel more natural. Again, Hernandez said he gravitates more toward the classic moves like hammer curls, tricep extensions, cable pull-throughs and bench presses.

Beyond working out, Hernandez made it abundantly clear that his abs are made in the kitchen.

“The trick is in the diet, that’s where it starts,” he said. Right now, Hernandez is doing round the clock eating, meaning he eats six meals a day, chowing down every two hours until 9 p.m. each night.

A typical day for him consists of four egg whites for breakfast along with a cap of apple cider vinegar. His next meal is five ounces of ground turkey with a side of asparagus. For lunch, Hernandez said, he eats tuna, sweet potatoes with a tablespoon of salsa “to make it palatable.”

In the afternoon Hernandez has a simple protein shake, followed by four ounces of chicken with a small salad. At night he finishes it off with five ounces of tilapia and four more egg whites.

“It’s real clean and it’s only one carb meal a day, which I eat right before I go to the gym,” he said.

What’s next for Hernandez? Right now he's focused on competing and continuing to motivate all people — adaptive or otherwise — through his social media posts (you can follow him on Instagram at @OneHandKing) .

“Never give up on your dreams,” he said. “Ever. And nothing is impossible. If you really want it, you’ll find a way.”
