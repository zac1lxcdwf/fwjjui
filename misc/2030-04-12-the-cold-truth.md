# The Cold Truth

Who will be more miserable on Sunday? The Packers and Giants at Lambeau Field, or the Patriots and Chargers in Gillette Stadium?

Game-time forecast in Green Bay: single digits with 10 mph winds. For Foxboro, Mass.: low 20s, winds around 20 mph, gusting to 40.

“Wind is the equalizer—it’s the worst thing a quarterback can face,” says Dan Fouts. The former San Diego Chargers quarterback says receivers, blockers, and passers alike will take the cold, sloppy conditions, but the wind throws their aerial attack off. “Because what you’re throwing isn’t a sphere.”

Of course, the cold’s a factor when it’s really, really cold, and Fouts knows this as well as any QB: He played in the second-coldest game in NFL history, a minus-9 degree AFC title bout with the Cincinnati Bengals in 1982, a game the Chargers lost, 27-7. Wind bursts that day made the temperature feel like it was minus-59, says the NFL Record and Fact Book.

Of course, men who work outside—quarterbacks and construction workers, garbage men and linemen—know how to deal with the cold. For Fouts and the Chargers, there was a heated bench on the sideline, hand-warmers, and big old mittens. For the rest of us, it means smart layering, staying dry, and avoiding those cut-through-you gusts of wind.

For those who don’t bother, there’s the familiar symptoms of exposure—shivering, teeth-chattering. Dr. Colin Grissom, M.D., a critical-care physician with the Intermountain Medical Center in Salt Lake City, explains what happens: You shiver to generate heat to maintain core body temp and to protect your vital organs. Meanwhile, your blood vessels in your less-essential arms and legs “clamp down to conserve heat in the core”—that is, you lose bloodflow to the very parts that power your outdoor activity.

If the exposed don’t run for shelter—and warmth and dry clothes—hypothermia rolls in: That’s the dangerous drop in temperature that leads to slurred speech, confused thought, and potentially (gulp), death.

It’d be good TV, but that probably won’t happen on Sunday. (Remember those heated benches?) Still, bloodflow won’t be optimal to the hands of QBs or receivers, and the cold could have other effects on the game.

“The ball will get more slippery,” Fouts says. “I don’t know if it’s ice crystals or what. It loses its tackiness. When it’s cold, the ball doesn’t have that little give to it.”

For more potential effects on equipment, quarterbacks, and guys like us, we gathered some guys who have had serious exposure to tell us what Favre, Brady, Manning, and Rivers have in store.

“No one thinks about the cold,” says Olympic biathelete Erich Wilbrecht. “Once the race or the game starts, there’s too much adrenaline pumping. It goes with the territory of being any kind of athlete.”

For some NFL players—the guys on offense—the cold actually plays to their mental advantage, says former Redskins and Broncos lineman Mark Schlereth.

“Defensive linemen hate to play in cold and nasty weather. Playing defensive line is predicated on being an athlete and getting their footing,” he says. Because the offense knows where they’re going and the defense has to react, the cold slows down the defense, creating more opportunities for the guys with the ball. “One of the mental advantages I thought I had was that I knew that D-linemen hated [the cold].”

No matter which side they’re on, though, “you can’t let it change the way you play,” says Fouts. “You’ve got enough things to think about … you don’t talk about it, you don’t think about it.”

Keeping Warm with Sleeves:

Linemen are known for going sleeveless when it’s frigid, a feat many fans think is just a flash of macho. Not so, says Schlereth.

“I never wore sleeves—as an offensive lineman, you don’t want to give something somebody to grab,” says the three-time Super Bowl champ. He did cover the exposed skin, though, to ward off the elements. “I would always coat my body with Vaseline. That helps keep the wind off you.”

When Fouts faced off against Cincinnati, he and Bengals QB Kenny Anderson were the only players without gloves.

“Most guys had scuba gloves or something that just kept their hands warm,” he says. “Those weren’t good enough to throw in … we didn’t have the gloves that they have today.”

But while the four QBs under center will likely glove up on Sunday, there’s been much hand-wringing over hand-covering in the NFL in past years: Steelers passer Ben Roethlisberger was harangued in the media after dropping a gloved playoff game to the Pats in his rookie year, and Panthers backup QB David Carr became more maligned for covering his throwing mitt this season.

A lot of that’s sports radio craziness, of course, but our panel of frozen pundits said there’s something to naked phalanges: Our Verizon lineman in Vermont, Chris Haselman, opts for fingerless. He’s a “splice service technician,” meaning he’s working with his fingers while perched atop ladders and phone poles along Lake Champlain.

“You just gotta keep ‘em moving,” he says of keeping feeling in his fingers. “Usually about 10 minutes, then you have to cover up, warm them back up.”

Wilbrecht, the skier and shooter, likes to feel his finger against the trigger of his gun. “I would cut a slit in my gloves and slide off just the index finger.”

On NFL sidelines, the benches aren’t just heated: Poles jutting from behind the bench act as perches and heaters for resting players’ helmets. And pity the player who forgets to perch his, says Mark Schlereth.

“The worst thing is to come off the sideline and your helmet gets cold,” he says. “The plastic becomes very hard, and it’s like putting on a piece of concrete.”

Players will do anything to keep their equipment—and thus their bodies—warm and dry. Schlereth recalls covering his shoes in silicone spray, while other linemen would wear thin plastic bags over their socks. It seems silly, but there’s good reason behind it: “Conductive loss of body heat is much greater if you’re wet,” says the Intermountain Medical Center’s Grissom. There is “up to 25 percent greater loss of body heat.”

Staying dry and having adequate insulation is the key, the doctor says, even if it means looking or feeling a little less manly. Because when it’s cold, the biggest, burliest men can humble themselves if the result’s a little toastier: “When I got to the stadium, there was a box in the middle of the locker room and guys were just grabbing things out of the box,” Collinsworth says of the ’82 AFC Championship. “I thought, ‘If it's free, it's for me.’ It turned out it was Hanes queen-size panty hose.

“All these big lineman, who never even wore sleeves, were fighting to get a pair,” he continues. The receiver claims he took a manlier route and didn’t wear them. By the end of the game, though, he might have been regretting that decision: “I wanted to win. I wanted to go to the Super Bowl. But I stopped caring about the final score and just wanted the game to end.”
