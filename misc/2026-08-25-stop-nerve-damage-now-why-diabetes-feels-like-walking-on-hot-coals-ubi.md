Explanation

Why Diabetes Makes It Feel Like You're Walking on Hot Coals

If you've ever described your feet as feeling like they're "walking on hot coals," you're not alone. This burning, tingling or searing pain is a hallmark of diabetic peripheral neuropathy—a type of nerve damage that affects up to half of people with diabetes over time. Below, we'll explore why diabetes can ignite this "hot‐coals" sensation, how to recognize it, and what you can do about it.

What Is Diabetic Neuropathy?

Diabetic neuropathy refers to nerve damage caused by persistently high blood sugar (glucose) levels. Over months and years, excess glucose injures nerves throughout your body—most often in the feet and legs first—leading to symptoms such as:

Burning, stabbing or electric‐shock pains

Tingling ("pins and needles")

Numbness or loss of sensation

Increased sensitivity to touch (allodynia)

When these symptoms feel like "walking on hot coals," it's usually the small nerve fibers in the skin that are most affected.

Why Does Nerve Damage Happen?

Several factors work together to damage nerves in diabetes:

• High Blood Sugar (Hyperglycemia) – Excess glucose enters nerve cells, disrupting normal metabolism. – It triggers inflammation and oxidative stress, which harm nerve fibers.

• Microvascular Changes – Tiny blood vessels that nourish nerves become thickened and narrowed. – Reduced blood flow starves nerves of oxygen and nutrients.

• Metabolic Derangements – Advanced glycation end‐products (AGEs) form, stiffening blood vessels and nerves. – Imbalances in insulin and growth factors impair nerve repair.

Over time, these processes lead to loss of the protective myelin sheath around nerves, slowed nerve conduction and the emergence of painful "positive" symptoms (burning, tingling) or "negative" ones (numbness).

How the "Hot Coals" Sensation Develops

Imagine tiny heat receptors in your feet firing off continuously, even without a hot surface to trigger them. That's essentially what happens when small nerve fibers become hyperexcitable:

Demyelination• Loss of myelin (the nerve's insulation) causes erratic electrical signals.• Nerves misinterpret normal touch or temperature as pain.

Ion Channel Dysfunction• Calcium and sodium channels on nerve membranes go haywire.• This leads to spontaneous nerve firing and burning sensations.

Central Sensitization• The spinal cord and brain become sensitized to incoming pain signals.• Even mild stimuli—like bedsheets brushing your feet—can feel excruciating.

Other Contributing Factors

• Duration of Diabetes – The longer you have diabetes, the greater your risk of neuropathy.

• Glycemic Control – Frequent highs and lows in blood sugar accelerate nerve damage.

• Coexisting Conditions – High blood pressure, high cholesterol and smoking worsen microvascular injury.

• Nutritional Deficiencies – Low levels of vitamin B12 and folate impair nerve health.

Recognizing the Signs

Burning feet are often the first clue, but neuropathy can progress and involve higher parts of the legs and hands. Key warning signs include:

A constant burning, tingling or shooting pain in your feet or calves.

Heightened sensitivity to touch or temperature (allodynia).

Areas of numbness or a "glove and stocking" loss of sensation.

Muscle weakness or difficulty walking.

Foot deformities or ulcers due to unnoticed injuries.

Diagnosing Diabetic Neuropathy

A doctor will usually start with a thorough history and physical exam, focusing on:

Sensory testing (monofilament, tuning fork)

Reflex checks (ankle and knee)

Assessment of gait and balance

Additional tests may include:

Nerve conduction studies (NCS) and electromyography (EMG)

Quantitative sensory testing (QST)

Skin biopsy to measure small fiber density

Early diagnosis is key to slowing progression and relieving pain.

Managing and Treating the "Hot Coals" Sensation

While nerve damage cannot always be reversed, many strategies can reduce pain and improve quality of life:

Blood Sugar Control• Aim for stable glucose levels through diet, exercise and medication adherence.• Work with your doctor to set realistic A1C targets.

Lifestyle and Home Remedies• Gentle daily foot care: wash, dry and moisturize your feet.• Wear well‐fitting shoes and avoid walking barefoot.• Incorporate low‐impact exercise (walking, swimming) to boost circulation.• Avoid smoking and limit alcohol, both of which worsen nerve damage.

Alternative and Complementary Therapies• Transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (TENS)• Mindfulness‐based stress reduction (MBSR) or meditation• Acupuncture (in some individuals)

Diabetic neuropathy can lead to serious complications, including foot ulcers, infections and even amputation if left unchecked. Be sure to speak to a doctor if you experience:

Unrelenting burning or shooting pain that disrupts sleep or daily activities

Loss of sensation or weakness in your feet or legs

Signs of infection, such as warmth, pus or red streaks

Your healthcare team can customize a treatment plan and screen for other causes of neuropathy, such as vitamin deficiencies or thyroid problems.

Key Takeaways

The "walking on hot coals" feeling in diabetes stems from damage to small nerve fibers and their blood supply.

High blood sugar, microvascular changes and metabolic byproducts all contribute to nerve injury.

Early recognition, tight glycemic control and targeted therapies can relieve burning pain and slow progression.
