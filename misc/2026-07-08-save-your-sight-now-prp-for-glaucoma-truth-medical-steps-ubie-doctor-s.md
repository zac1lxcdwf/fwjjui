Reviewed by Yoshinori Abe, MD

Internal Medicine

Panretinal photocoagulation (PRP) treats neovascular glaucoma by using targeted peripheral retinal laser to reduce ischemia-driven VEGF production. This causes abnormal iris and angle blood vessels to regress, reopens the drainage angle, lowers intraocular pressure (IOP), and prevents synechiae—helping preserve vision. PRP is an outpatient procedure commonly combined with anti-VEGF injections and IOP-lowering medications to stabilize retinal disease and reduce the need for surgery.

Key considerations include the risk of peripheral vision loss, the importance of thorough evaluation and follow-up, and the potential need for additional treatments.

How PRP for Glaucoma Saves Your Sight: Reality and Medically Approved Next Steps

Glaucoma is a leading cause of irreversible vision loss. In a subtype called neovascular glaucoma, new, fragile blood vessels grow on the iris and drainage angle, blocking fluid outflow and raising intraocular pressure (IOP). Pan-retinal photocoagulation (PRP for glaucoma) is an established laser treatment that can halt this process and help preserve your vision.

What Is PRP for Glaucoma?

• Pan-retinal photocoagulation (PRP) uses laser burns across the peripheral retina• It is primarily indicated in proliferative diabetic retinopathy or retinal vein occlusion—conditions that fuel neovascular glaucoma• By treating a large retinal area, PRP reduces the retina's oxygen demand and cuts production of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), the key driver of unwanted new blood vessels

How PRP Works to Preserve Sight

VEGF Reduction• Ischemic retina produces excess VEGF• PRP destroys patches of peripheral retina, lowering VEGF levels• Existing abnormal vessels regress, and new growth slows

Pressure Control• Neovascular membranes in the drainage angle block fluid outflow• As vessels shrink, the angle re-opens slightly, improving IOP• Lower IOP means less stress on the optic nerve

Preventing Synechiae• Fibrous tissue can form permanent adhesions (synechiae) that seal the angle• Early PRP reduces inflammation and vessel proliferation, preventing angle closure

Benefits of PRP for Glaucoma

Rapid regression of iris and angle neovascularization

Decreased need for glaucoma surgery in some cases

Stabilization of retinal disease that threatens central vision

Integration with anti-VEGF injections for a combined approach

What to Expect During and After PRP

Pre-Treatment Evaluation

Comprehensive eye exam including IOP measurement and retinal imaging

Discussion of your medical history (especially diabetes control)

Procedure

Topical anesthesia (eye drops) or mild sedation

15–30 minutes of laser applications to the peripheral retina

You may feel mild discomfort or flashing lights

Post-Treatment Care

Anti-inflammatory eye drops for 1–2 weeks

Possible temporary increase in IOP—monitoring is essential

Follow-up visits at 1 week, 1 month, and as advised

Risks and Considerations

While PRP is generally safe, it carries some risks:

Peripheral visual field loss or night-vision difficulty

Temporary swelling or inflammation

Rarely, macular edema (swelling at the central retina)

Medically Approved Next Steps

Comprehensive Evaluation

Confirm neovascular glaucoma diagnosis with gonioscopy, OCT, and fluorescein angiography

Assess overall eye health and coexisting diabetic or vascular conditions

Medical Management

Start or adjust IOP-lowering eye drops (prostaglandin analogs, beta-blockers, carbonic anhydrase inhibitors)

Optimize systemic control of diabetes and hypertension

Anti-VEGF Therapy

Intravitreal injections (bevacizumab, ranibizumab, aflibercept) to rapidly shrink neovessels

Often combined with PRP for sustained VEGF suppression

Laser Treatment (PRP for Glaucoma)

Schedule PRP promptly to address widespread ischemia

Tailor laser intensity and spot count to individual retinal findings

Surgical Options

If IOP remains uncontrolled, consider glaucoma drainage devices or cyclophotocoagulation

Trabeculectomy is less favored in neovascular cases due to higher failure rates

Lifelong Monitoring

Regular IOP checks, visual field testing, and retinal exams

Early detection of complications or disease progression
