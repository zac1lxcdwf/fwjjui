Explanation

Prolactin is a hormone made by your pituitary gland, a small pea-sized organ at the base of your brain. Its main job is to help with milk production after childbirth. However, sometimes prolactin levels can become too high—a condition called hyperprolactinemia. Doctors test for high prolactin when they suspect it may be the underlying cause of a range of health issues. Understanding why this test matters can help you recognize symptoms early, explore treatment options, and work with your healthcare provider to find the best path forward.

What Is Prolactin and Why Does It Matter?

Prolactin plays several roles in your body beyond lactation:

Promotes breast development during pregnancy

Helps trigger milk production after delivery

Influences reproductive hormones in both women and men

May affect mood, immune function, and metabolism

While prolactin is essential for new mothers, too much of it—high prolactin—can disrupt normal hormone balance. That's why doctors pay attention to prolactin levels when patients report specific symptoms.

Common Causes of High Prolactin

High prolactin isn't always caused by a serious disease. Possible triggers include:

Medication side effects: Certain antidepressants, antipsychotics, blood pressure drugs and even opiates can boost prolactin

Benign pituitary tumors (prolactinomas): Small growths on the pituitary gland that secrete extra prolactin

Thyroid problems: Underactive thyroid (hypothyroidism) can raise prolactin levels

Stress and sleep: Physical or emotional stress, as well as poor sleep patterns, may cause temporary spikes

Chest wall irritation: Herpes zoster, surgical scars or tight clothing against the chest can signal the pituitary to release more prolactin

Because high prolactin can stem from many sources, doctors may order tests to pinpoint the exact cause.

Signs and Symptoms to Watch For

Symptoms of high prolactin differ between women and men, but there is some overlap:

Women

Irregular or absent menstrual periods (amenorrhea)

Milk production unrelated to pregnancy or breastfeeding (galactorrhea)

Painful intercourse due to vaginal dryness

Infertility or difficulty conceiving

Men

Erectile dysfunction or low libido

Reduced muscle mass and strength

Enlarged breast tissue (gynecomastia)

Infertility due to low sperm count

Both sexes may experience headaches or vision changes if a pituitary tumor grows large enough to press on nearby nerves. If you notice any of these signs, your doctor may recommend a prolactin test.

Why Doctors Order a Prolactin Test

A prolactin blood test is a simple way to measure how much of this hormone is circulating in your body. Here's why your healthcare provider may suggest it:

Evaluate unexplained menstrual changes or infertility

Investigate spontaneous milk flow (galactorrhea)

Assess sexual dysfunction in men

Rule out pituitary adenomas when patients report headaches or vision changes

Monitor treatment effectiveness if you already have a known prolactinoma

By measuring prolactin, doctors can decide whether further imaging (like an MRI of the pituitary) or other tests are needed.

How the Prolactin Test Works

Preparing for and taking a prolactin test is straightforward:

Fast if instructed: Some labs may ask you to avoid food for several hours before the draw.

Time it right: Prolactin levels peak during sleep and early morning, so blood is often drawn in the morning, about 2–3 hours after waking.

Rest beforehand: Exercise and stress can transiently raise prolactin. You may be asked to sit quietly for 15–30 minutes before the sample is taken.

Blood draw: A healthcare professional cleans your arm, inserts a needle into a vein, and collects a small blood sample.

Results are usually back within a day or two. Normal prolactin ranges vary slightly by lab but generally fall between 2–29 ng/mL in women and 2–18 ng/mL in men.

Interpreting High Prolactin Results

High prolactin can be mild, moderate or very significant. Doctors interpret your results alongside symptoms and other tests:

Mild elevation (30–50 ng/mL): Often linked to medications, stress, or mild thyroid issues

Moderate elevation (50–200 ng/mL): May signal a small prolactinoma or more pronounced thyroid dysfunction

High elevation (>200 ng/mL): Strongly suggests a prolactin-secreting pituitary adenoma

If your prolactin is high, your doctor may order:

Thyroid function tests (TSH, free T4)

MRI of the pituitary gland

Additional hormonal assays (e.g., LH, FSH, estrogen or testosterone levels)

These steps help rule out other conditions and confirm the diagnosis.

Treatment Options for High Prolactin

Managing hyperprolactinemia aims to normalize prolactin levels, relieve symptoms, and address the underlying cause:

Medications

Dopamine agonists (e.g., cabergoline, bromocriptine) are first-line treatments. They decrease prolactin production, shrink pituitary tumors and restore normal function in most people.

Thyroid therapy

If hypothyroidism is the cause, thyroid hormone replacement often brings prolactin back to normal.

Surgery or radiation

Reserved for individuals who cannot tolerate medication or have tumors resistant to drugs. Surgical removal of a pituitary adenoma usually improves symptoms, and radiation may be used if surgery is incomplete.

Regular follow-up visits and blood tests ensure your treatment is working and side effects are managed.

Living with High Prolactin

Once diagnosed, most people respond well to treatment and see an improvement in symptoms. Lifestyle changes and self-care can also help:

Manage stress through relaxation techniques or counseling

Maintain regular sleep patterns to keep hormone levels steady

Follow up with your healthcare provider as recommended

Open communication with your doctor about any new or worsening symptoms is key to long-term success.

When to Speak to a Doctor

While high prolactin is often treatable, some situations require prompt medical attention:

Sudden, severe headaches or vision loss

Pregnancy-like milk production without recent childbirth

Complete absence of menstrual periods for three months or more

Erectile dysfunction or infertility in men

If you experience any of these signs, please speak to a doctor as soon as possible. Only a healthcare professional can determine the seriousness of your condition and recommend the appropriate tests and treatments.

Key Takeaways

Prolactin is essential for lactation but can cause health problems when levels are too high.

Symptoms of hyperprolactinemia include menstrual changes, galactorrhea, sexual dysfunction and headaches.
