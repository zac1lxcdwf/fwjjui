# I’m a Mom of 3 — Here Are Some of My Favorite Tips to Help Reduce My Children’s Screen Time

I’m a Mom of 3 — Here Are Some of My Favorite Tips to Help Reduce My Children’s Screen Time

As a busy, work-from-home mom of three kids ages 2 to 10, I know exactly how easy it is to depend on screens as a distraction for my children, so I can get things done. Unfortunately, though, our kids as a whole may be getting far too much screen time.

[1]

[2]

“Excessive screen time displaces sleep, physical activity, face-to-face interaction, and boredom, all of which are essential for healthy child development,” says Nidhi Gupta, MD, a pediatric endocrinologist in Franklin, Tennessee, and founder of the Phreedom Foundation, which encourages healthy and responsible device use for all ages. “Social media also fuels comparison, anxiety, and emotional dysregulation, while blue light exposure and stimulating content disrupt sleep and circadian rhythms. Simply put, children’s brains are being exposed to constant stimulation without enough time for recovery and real-world engagement.”

I spoke to several pediatricians, psychiatrists, and mental health specialists about how to meaningfully reduce screen time in children. Plus, Everyday Health editors and contributors — and most importantly, their children — tested several screen-free activities that can serve as alternatives. Below, you'll find some of the Everyday Health team's favorite boredom-busting screen substitutes, as well as advice for screen time management from the experts I interviewed.

Why Limit Screen Time for Kids?

Another real concern is that we’re using screens to replace transitions that were once handled by parents, like settling children down for a nap or calming them when they're frustrated, says Carla Allan, PhD, division chief of psychology at Phoenix Children's Hospital. “We’re outsourcing a child’s development of self-regulation to a device that can’t teach them the emotional tools they need to live healthy lives,” she says.

Too much screen time can also negatively affect eye health. “As a pediatric ophthalmologist, I see this firsthand,” says David G. Hunter, MD, PhD, ophthalmologist-in-chief at Boston Children's Hospital and a vice chair of ophthalmology at Harvard Medical School. “First, when a child spends too much time staring at screens up close, they overuse their focusing muscles, leading to eye strain and other problems. Second, the screens are so enticing that kids can actually forget to blink, which causes the eyes to dry out, leading to irritation and compounding the eye strain.”

However, screen use can affect children differently based on a number of factors. “The impact of screen use can vary significantly depending on a child’s age, developmental stage, and underlying mental health profile, including conditions such as attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) or anxiety," says Andrea Diaz Stransky, MD, a Durham, North Carolina–based, board-certified child and adolescent psychiatrist and clinical reviewer at Emora Health.

Should Children Be Completely Screen-Free?

The experts agree: Expecting children to be completely screen-free is unrealistic. Instead, Dr. Allan recommends that parents focus on three important things when it comes to screen time: quality, quantity, and timing.

Parents can set limits on the quality of content their children are exposed to, the amount of screen time they let their kids have each day, and the times their kids allowed to use screens. “For example, while children may sit more calmly with a device at dinner, it interferes with their ability to listen to their own biological hunger cues, which can lead to disordered eating, like overeating or under-eating,” she says.

“Interactive screen use close to bedtime has been associated with poorer sleep onset and sleep quality in youth, which can in turn affect attention, mood, and behavior," says Dr. Diaz Stransky. Mindless scrolling or social media exposure also can be damaging. But other types of screen time can be beneficial, like educational content, she says.

Screens can also be used to communicate with family via video calls or for family bonding, for example, by watching a movie or a TV show together. Many children are also expected to complete schoolwork on a computer, and it's important that they learn how to use technology.

“Our kids live in the real world, and the reality is that screens are everywhere,” says Dr. Redovian. “If we simply expect them not to use them, we're putting them at a disadvantage.”

Are There Age-Based Recommendations for Daily Screen Time?

The American Academy of Pediatrics used to prescribe specific time recommendations based on age. However, Andrew Fishman, LCSW, a Chicago-based mental health professional who specializes in treating video game addiction in adolescents, says: “They just updated their recommendations to a more nuanced approach. They replaced specific recommended hours per day with a list of criteria to consider.” These criteria are called “The 5 C's of Media Use.” The 5 C's are:

Child How does your child in particular respond to screen usage? Some kids may be able to handle more, but some kids may require stricter limitations for mental and physical well-being.

Content Content quality should also be considered. As Fishman puts it, "Evidence suggests that TikTok and other short-form video platforms are bad for developing brains, but no one would suggest that a nature documentary has the same effect on a teenager.”

How Can Parents Get Their Children On Board?

Speaking from personal experience here: Getting "buy-in" from your kids about reducing screen time can be a real challenge. Every summer, we try to do a two-week screen detox, and my kids always have a blast. I also asked the experts about this, and they provided some really helpful advice.

“First and foremost, we’ve got to model good behavior,” says Allan. We as adults need to be willing to set the example of responsible screen usage for our children. And Redovian says, "If you're asking your kid to put down the video game, they're going to feel some kind of way if they see you playing Candy Crush on your phone while they're supposed to do something without electronics.”

Remember to talk to your kids about it, too. “Involve them in the discussion as much as you can, and remember to keep an open mind. Screen time might not be the actual problem,” says Fishman. It's possible your child may be spending more time on screens because they're feeling stressed or burned out. “Once you understand their perspective, you can work with them to make a plan together that might or might not involve reducing screen time.”
