Facing a Salpingectomy? Why Surgery Is Required and Your Medical Next Steps

If your doctor has recommended a salpingectomy, you may be feeling overwhelmed. It's normal to have questions about why this surgery is necessary, what it involves, and what it means for your health and fertility.

This guide explains what a salpingectomy is, when it's required, what to expect before and after surgery, and what your next medical steps should be—using clear, straightforward language.

What Is a Salpingectomy?

A salpingectomy is a surgical procedure to remove one or both fallopian tubes.

Your fallopian tubes connect your ovaries to your uterus. They play a key role in pregnancy because they're where fertilization typically occurs.

There are two main types:

Unilateral salpingectomy – removal of one fallopian tube

Bilateral salpingectomy – removal of both fallopian tubes

The type you need depends on your medical condition.

Why Is a Salpingectomy Required?

A salpingectomy is usually recommended when keeping the fallopian tube would put your health at risk. The most common reasons include:

1. Ectopic Pregnancy (Most Common Reason)

An ectopic pregnancy happens when a fertilized egg implants outside the uterus—most often in a fallopian tube.

This is a medical emergency because:

The tube cannot support a growing pregnancy

The tube can rupture

Internal bleeding can occur

It can become life-threatening

If the tube is severely damaged or ruptured, a salpingectomy is often the safest and fastest treatment.

If you are experiencing symptoms like sharp or severe abdominal pain, shoulder pain, vaginal bleeding during early pregnancy, or dizziness and fainting, you should assess your risk immediately using a free Ectopic Pregnancy symptom checker to understand whether your symptoms require emergency care. This does not replace urgent medical attention—if you suspect an ectopic pregnancy, seek emergency treatment immediately.

2. Severe Tubal Damage or Infection

Conditions like:

Pelvic inflammatory disease (PID)

Severe scarring

Chronic infection

can permanently damage a fallopian tube. If the tube is beyond repair and causing ongoing problems, removal may be necessary.

3. Fallopian Tube Cancer or High Cancer Risk

Although rare, cancer can develop in the fallopian tubes. In some cases, women at high genetic risk of ovarian cancer (such as BRCA mutations) may choose a preventive bilateral salpingectomy to reduce risk.

Research shows that some ovarian cancers may actually begin in the fallopian tubes. Removing them can significantly lower cancer risk.

4. Hydrosalpinx

This is when a fallopian tube fills with fluid due to blockage. It can:

Lower IVF success rates

How Is a Salpingectomy Performed?

Most salpingectomy procedures are done using laparoscopic (minimally invasive) surgery.

Laparoscopic Salpingectomy:

Small incisions in the abdomen

A tiny camera is inserted

Surgical instruments remove the tube

Usually same-day discharge

Open Surgery (Laparotomy):

Larger incision

Used in emergencies (such as ruptured ectopic pregnancy)

Longer recovery time

Your surgeon will decide the safest approach based on your condition.

What Happens Before Surgery?

Before a salpingectomy, your medical team may:

Perform blood tests

Confirm pregnancy status (if applicable)

Use ultrasound imaging

Review your medical history

Discuss anesthesia risks

Explain fertility implications

If this is an emergency (like a ruptured ectopic pregnancy), surgery may happen quickly to protect your life.

What Are the Risks of Salpingectomy?

Every surgery carries some risk. With a salpingectomy, possible risks include:

Bleeding

Infection

Injury to nearby organs

Reaction to anesthesia

Scar tissue formation

In emergency cases, risks may be higher due to internal bleeding.

That said, when medically indicated, the benefits of surgery usually outweigh the risks.

Recovery depends on the type of surgery.

After Laparoscopic Salpingectomy:

Mild to moderate abdominal pain for a few days

Shoulder pain from surgical gas (temporary)

Return to light activity within 1–2 weeks

Full recovery in about 2–4 weeks

What Does a Salpingectomy Mean for Fertility?

This is one of the most common concerns.

If One Tube Is Removed (Unilateral Salpingectomy):

You can still get pregnant naturally

The remaining tube can often pick up eggs from either ovary

Fertility may be slightly reduced, depending on overall health

If Both Tubes Are Removed (Bilateral Salpingectomy):

Natural pregnancy is no longer possible

In vitro fertilization (IVF) is still an option

Ovaries continue to function normally

You will still produce hormones and have menstrual cycles

If future pregnancy is important to you, discuss fertility preservation and reproductive options with your doctor.

Emotional Impact: What You May Be Feeling

It's common to feel:

Shock (especially after emergency surgery)

Grief (particularly after ectopic pregnancy)

Anxiety about fertility

Fear about future health

These feelings are valid. A salpingectomy may be medically necessary, but that doesn't mean it's emotionally easy.

Consider:

Speaking with a counselor

Joining a support group

Talking openly with your healthcare provider

Mental health care is part of recovery.

Key Takeaways

A salpingectomy removes one or both fallopian tubes.

The most common reason is ectopic pregnancy.

It may also be required for infection, damage, cancer risk, or fertility issues.

Most procedures are minimally invasive.

Recovery is usually manageable.

Fertility depends on whether one or both tubes are removed.

In emergency cases, it can be life-saving.

Final Thoughts

Facing a salpingectomy can feel frightening. But in many cases, this surgery is performed to protect your health, prevent life-threatening complications, or reduce cancer risk.

The most important next step is to speak to a doctor about your specific situation—especially if you have symptoms that could be serious or life-threatening.

If you're experiencing concerning symptoms and want to better understand your risk, use a free Ectopic Pregnancy symptom checker for preliminary information—but always seek immediate medical attention if symptoms are severe.

Your health and safety come first. With the right information and medical guidance, you can move forward with clarity and confidence.
