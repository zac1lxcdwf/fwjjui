WHEN YOU HEAR “pelvic floor”—if you’ve heard of it at all—there’s a good chance you’ve written it off as something only women have to deal with, particularly after giving birth. Although that’s true, men absolutely have a pelvic floor, and when it experiences dysfunction, it can lead to a host of health problems, including different types of erectile dysfunction and sexual dissatisfaction.

Men’s health—general and sexual—are closely connected to the function of the pelvic floor, says Susie Gronski, PT, DPT, CSCS, a pelvic floor physical therapist and sexuality counselor in Asheville, North Carolina. (Her colleagues in the field call her the guru of the male pelvic floor.) Found in the lower abdominal region, between the pubic bone and the tailbone, the pelvic floor reaches front to back, and between the hips, side to side. Think of it as a hammock of muscles, which holds organs, including the bladder, prostate, and rectum, says Lauren Garges, PT, DPT, a pelvic floor physical therapist with St. Luke’s University Health Network in Bethlehem, Pennsylvania.

Men also have a set of pelvic floor muscles called the urogenital triangle, which play vital roles in achieving and maintaining erections, and supporting healthy ejaculation, Gronski says. (FYI: Women also have a urogenital triangle, although the muscles that make up this part of the body differ from those of their male counterparts.)

Understanding where the pelvic floor is, what its role is, and how to keep it healthy is crucial for men of all ages, but especially for those who have had surgeries or cancer treatments in the lower back or in the pelvic region (prostate, rectum, bladder, anus, colon). And, as men get older, their prostates increase in size, which can contribute to pelvic floor dysfunction, an umbrella term that includes a number of diagnoses, Garges says. Basically, it means the pelvic floor muscles are not working properly, and that can lead to health problems, including urinary and fecal incontinence, urinary frequency and urgency, erectile and ejaculatory dysfunction, scrotal pain, and pelvic pain.

When it comes to sexual health and function, specifically, if the pelvic floor muscles are too tight or too weak, they aren’t able to bring or hold blood flow to the penis, which is required for achieving and maintaining an erection, says Kylene Logorda, PT, DPT, also with St. Luke’s University Health Network.

How to Suss Out Your Pelvic Floor Strength

GARGES OFFERS A simple at-home way to determine if your pelvic floor is too weak or too tight:

Step 1: Assume position.

Sit on a large yoga ball or a rolled-up hand towel. You should feel pressure on the perineum, the space between the genitals and anus.

Step 2: Contract, then release.

Slowly contract the muscles you would use to stop the flow of urine or gas, as if you’re trying to vacuum up a marble. Then, try to release those muscles, moving downward like an elevator.

Step 3: Assess.

If nothing happens, your muscles may be as tight or contracted as they can be. If you can’t relax the muscles, that’s also a sign of tension. A weak pelvic floor will make it challenging or impossible to contract the muscles at all.

How Pelvic Floor Physical Therapy Can Improve Your Sexual and Overall Health

BECAUSE THE PELVIC floor comprises skeletal muscles—like your quadriceps, hamstrings, biceps—physical therapy, stretching, and strength training are effective ways to fix common problems associated with pelvic floor dysfunction, like ED and premature ejaculation, Logorda says.

And although taking medication for erectile dysfunction can be effective in some cases, experts tell Men’s Health that medication doesn’t necessarily get at the root of the problem, and it may not alleviate certain types of erectile dysfunction. Mainstream erectile dysfunction medication does not help with ejaculatory dysfunction, Gronski says, including premature or delayed ejaculation.

Pelvic floor physical therapy really helps with erectile dysfunction in a “more holistic” way, Logorda adds. “There aren’t side effects like with medication. Instead of putting a bandaid over it, physical therapy addresses the root cause of the symptoms.”

In fact, pelvic floor physical therapy can not only help counteract erectile dysfunction and premature ejaculation, but also increase overall sexual satisfaction, according to a 2025 study published in the International Journal of Impotence Research.

Pelvic Floor Physical Therapy Exercises to Try

LIKE MANY HEALTH-related concerns, there isn’t a one-size-fits-all treatment for pelvic floor dysfunction. The Kegel exercise, for example, is popular to strengthen pelvic floor muscles, especially in women, but it’s not necessarily right for everyone, especially if your pelvic floor is already too tight. Your best bet, experts say, is to work with a pelvic floor physical therapist to diagnose your individual issues and develop a treatment plan.

That said, Gronski recommends this focused breathing exercise to promote breath awareness and mind-body connection. Doing this a few times a week can benefit your overall health and wellness, regardless of pelvic floor concerns:

Inhale through your nose, filling your belly and chest with air. Imagine you’re filling your scrotum up like a balloon, Gronski says.

On the exhale, gently contract the pelvic floor muscles—those you would use to stop the flow of urine, or try to move your penis up and down—and then relax again on the inhale—Gronski calls this “booty hole breathing.”

The Effectiveness Of High-Tech Treatments

YOU MAY HAVE heard of various technologies that tout their ability to treat pelvic floor dysfunction, particularly erectile dysfunction. Big names include EMSELLA, PelviTouch, and a new male masturbator, F2S, from Lelo.

The F2s claims it can help with premature ejaculation with guided kegel exercises. Although Garges hasn’t personally used the device with her patients, she reviewed the exercises and surmises that “the guided pelvic floor training, contraction, and relaxation would be a good adjunct with physical therapy.”

As for the other designer technology, so to speak, Gronski is wary at best. Emsella and PelviTouch use a magnetic field, just like an MRI with slightly less force, to stimulate the nerves and muscles without skin contact, like a biofeedback tool. They claim to improve functional training and strength, Gronski says, likening these claims to saying that if you slap on an electrode to your biceps and stimulate the muscle, it’ll translate to functional activity without having to actually train. A spokesperson from PelviTouch told Men’s Health by email that its technology is “is not an alternative to physiotherapy, rather it is a tool to be included in a rehabilitation protocol.”

The research proving these claims is wanting, Gronski contends, and more independent studies need to be conducted. Currently, the research on these technologies contains small studies, many of which are funded by the manufacturers, she says. A spokesperson for Emsella told Men’s Health by email: “We recognize the concerns raised about the research landscape; however, it is important to clarify that in addition to sponsor-funded research, there are also independent, peer-reviewed studies, including randomized controlled trials with control groups that support Emsella’s efficacy and safety.”
