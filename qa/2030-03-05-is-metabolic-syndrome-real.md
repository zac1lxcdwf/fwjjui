# Is Metabolic Syndrome Real?

Sick of all the syndromes? There may be one less to worry about.

The American Diabetes Association and European Association for the Study of Diabetes say the condition called metabolic syndrome is "poorly defined, inconsistently used and in need of further research to help understand whether and how it should be treated."

In addition, the groups in the September issue of Diabetes Care and Diabetologia say doctors shouldn't diagnose the syndrome or try to treat it until "until the science behind it is clear."

"Doing so misleads the patient into believing he or she has a unique disease," Richard Kahn, the ADA's chief scientific and medical officer, said in a statement.

"What they really have are well-known cardiovascular risk factors. The combination of risk factors does not add up to a more significant or higher cardiovascular risk than the individual components."

Metabolic syndrome, if it exists, is characterized by excessive fat around the abdomen, blood fat disorders that lead to plaque buildup, high blood pressure and insulin resistance or glucose intolerance, among other things, according to the American Heart Association (AHA).

But people with these problems may not want to get bogged down in the debate because the symptoms alone carry risks.

"I don't think we should allow semantics to get in the way of disease prevention efforts," says Dr. David L. Katz, a Men's Health advisory board member who's also an associate professor of public health and director of the Prevention Research Center at the Yale University School of Medicine.

We know that tens of millions of Americans are insulin resistant because it can be measured, Dr. Katz says. And insulin resistance tends to occur when people gain weight around their stomachs. This in turn produces high blood pressure, high triglycerides, and several related metabolic abnormalities. If left unchecked, it all leads to diabetes.

Dr. Katz is even more aggressive than groups like the American Heart Association when it comes to defining metabolic syndrome. His description: "If your waist circumference is high -- period," he says. "The value in this is that it sensitizes those at early stages of risk to their risk, and invites them to do something about it."

"Debate the definition of metabolic syndrome? Fine," he adds. "Debate its existence? A distraction, and a disservice to the public health."

The AHA is among the many sources promoting the idea that metabolic syndrome is a growing problem. It has extensive information on its Web site about the condition.

For instance, it recently put out a press release saying that teenagers exposed to tobacco smoke are at a higher risk of metabolic syndrome. The AHA says it's estimated that 47 million American adults have the syndrome.

At least one drug maker also hopes to profit from the syndrome. Sanofi-Aventis wants to market its new anti-obesity drug Acomplia as a treatment for the syndrome, Reuters reports. And a search for "metabolic syndrome" on Google produces 550,000 results.

But even the AHA notes that on its Web site that "there are no well-accepted criteria for diagnosing the metabolic syndrome."

Still, the diabetes groups didn't dissuade the AHA from its belief in the syndrome. In a press release Friday, the group says that "identification of patients with the metabolic syndrome in clinical practice assists health professionals in the management and reduction of cardiovascular risk."

The two diabetes groups say the pieces of the syndrome are simply risk factors for cardiovascular disease. "But there is no combination of risk factors that boosts a person's cardiovascular risk beyond the sum of the parts, or constitutes a separate disease," Dr. Ele Ferrannini, European Association for the Study of Diabetes president, said in a statement.
