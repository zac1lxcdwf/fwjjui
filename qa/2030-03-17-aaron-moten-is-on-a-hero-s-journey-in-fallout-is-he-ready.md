# Aaron Moten Is On a Hero’s Journey in ‘Fallout.’ Is He Ready?

OF ALL THE post-apocalyptic shows on all of television, Aaron Moten just had to join Fallout. The Prime Video show, based on a popular series of video games, is a delightfully unhinged pastiche of genres. It’s set in a world where the Cold War never quite ended, freezing America in a 1960s-style retro-futurist society, until shadowy corporate and political powers deliberately triggered a nuclear annihilation. Moten plays Maximus, a man who was born in the aftermath and seeks purpose and connection in that lonely world. He finds it when he meets Lucy (Ella Purnell), a Pollyannaish “vault dweller” who leaves the self-sustaining bunker she grew up in, and the Ghoul (Walton Goggins), a onetime Golden Age Hollywood icon turned mutated gunslinger. Though they spend the bulk of the show’s second season siloed in their own storylines, next week’s finale may finally bring them back together.

But if all that time apart had one benefit, it was the way it allowed the series to showcase its genre-bending style. “I’m such an old movie kind of guy,” Moten says, “and I feel like everybody’s been borrowing from everyone. It’s been like that for 50 years. Modern filmmakers have just been borrowing from French New Wave and Russian and Japanese filmmakers and so on. And because the Fallout games are so uniquely strange, we can reference just about anything. It’s such an exciting way to tell a story.”

The 36-year-old actor goes on to draw parallels between the various storylines on the show and the different sandboxes they get to play in: For a time, Maximus throws his lot in with the Brotherhood of Steel, a military operation that cosplays as something out of a chivalric epic, except with robots instead of steeds. The Ghoul roams the wasteland in a one-man Western, but in flashbacks to his former life as Cooper Howard, he’s ensnared in a political thriller that slowly unravels the origins of the post-apocalyptic world the show is set in. Even the cast of offbeat characters still stuck in their vault are engaged in a dark parody of noir mysteries. Meanwhile, Purnell, more than anyone else, gets to dip in and out of different genres as Lucy crosses paths with fellow travelers. Kyle MacLachlan, who plays Lucy’s father, does his own uniquely Kyle MacLachlan thing.

“We get to do so much, and it’s amazing that this world can accommodate all of that,” Moten says. And as the second season wraps up, with a third already in the works, Moten promises a lot more weirdness to come. He also took the chance to reflect on Maximus’s hero’s journey (including its many unheroic moments), what it says about the themes of masculinity and sense of self, and getting to watch his castmates learn to don the 60-pound practical power armor he’s had to wear for two seasons.

MEN’S HEALTH: There’s a lot going on in Fallout—a lot of lore, action, and wild practical effects to contend with. Did you feel more prepared to take it on in the second season?

AARON MOTEN: I wish! The show is so massive in its scope that it always feels like you see the mountain before you start climbing it. But all of us—Ella, Walton, [executive producers] Jonathan Nolan and Lisa Joy, and our showrunners [Graham Wagner and Geneva Robertson-Dworet]—wanted to do the thing like we had never done it before and like we may not get the opportunity to do it again. It’s stressful, but always really thrilling.

MH: On top of everything, the show shoots on film, which is unusual.

AM: The first season was my first opportunity as an actor to work on film. Everything had been digital for me, and film is just so different. It’s the difference between someone pushing a button on a digital camera and telling you to go play versus someone shooting you on film and knowing in the back of your mind that we’re spending money every second the camera is rolling. It makes us work a little more economically. I actually found it to be a really healthy approach to TV, when you have to move quickly, to know you’ve got to have your ducks in a row before action gets called.

MH: The practical approach to the filming and effects and costumes really adds to the texture of the world too.

AM: It’s just an ideology passed down by Jonathan Nolan. With film, you’re capturing the actual light of the day, and when you’re filming in actual locations it just looks better. And, gosh, the practical stuff does so much for the quality of our work. It’s easier for postproduction, yes, but it gives us actors more of an understanding of what we’re working with in a scene.

“We talk about MORAL COMPASSES, but at a certain point, it’s also a STORY ABOUT SURVIVAL.”

MH: This season, other actors, like Kyle and Walton, got to wear the power armor and experience a little of what you’ve done a lot of over these two seasons—which is try to act in this hulking costume. How’d that go for them?

AM: It’s tough. It’s just really heavy. But there’s a reason for the weight—we’ve thrown it through walls, we’ve put it in bodies of water, we’ve rolled it around in the sand. You want the thing to not fall apart. It’s about 50 to 60 pounds. It feels like having an 11- or 12-year-old on your shoulders and then being told: “Now act!”

But it was exciting to see these other actors go through it—Kyle, Walton, and Kumail Nanjiani, who joins us for a few episodes, too. The first time Walton had to try it on, he called me after and said, “Hey, man, I don’t know how you do it!” I give what pointers I can, when I can. The biggest one is that the first time you put it on, you want to dance. You want to move around and test it out because it’s so cool. But you’ve got to pace yourself, because you’re going to be in that thing for three to six hours, and about 10 to 15 minutes in, the weight of it starts to hit you. It’s hard to breathe, it’s a lot of weight on top of your shoulders, and you can start feeling trapped or a little claustrophobic because you can’t let yourself out of it. You have to be let out of it. It’s bolted together, so someone on the crew has to pry you out if you need a break.

MH: There was a flashback this season to Maximus’s childhood in Shady Sands before it was destroyed, which is a departure from the games. We meet his parents and get a glimpse into their family’s life on the settlement. How much of that backstory did you know in the first season?

AM: I knew all of it—I thought it felt like a little bit of a secret. The only clue we gave in the first season was the fridge, right? We had a young boy coming out of the fridge, and we had Thaddeus say that it seems like Maximus came from someplace better. But getting to see it all written out and then shown on-screen was really powerful for me to see—and very, very helpful to the experience of playing the character.

MH: Something clicks once you see his parents and see how they contributed to their community and how they sacrificed themselves to save Maximus. You understand why he strives so hard, but also why he struggles to stack up to the heroic memory of his parents.

AM: Throughout the first season, we were always thinking of how we can let Maximus, in his head, connect with Lucy through his backstory. He hasn’t seen another person like Lucy—someone who reminds him of the goodness that can exist in people, a willingness to help others and to do good. It reminds him of where he came from.

MH: By the end of the first season, Maximus had stumbled into being a heroic figure to the Brotherhood of Steel. This season, he vacillates between feeling like an impostor and perhaps buying his own hype a bit too much and eventually ditching the entire moral code of the Brotherhood. How did you trace that evolution?

AM: There are so many moments in the first season where we find this character getting in trouble by trying to do the right thing. We talk about moral compasses, but at a certain point, it’s also a story about survival. Take Titus—here’s a guy who was going to send Maximus into a cave to die. And when Maximus has the opportunity to help him, probably at the expense of his own life down the line, he just can’t do it. He’s dabbling in these difficult choices: If I do the right thing, I’ll end up dead. Maximus tries to fit in by being different versions of himself depending on who he’s talking to, but the times that we see Maximus for who he truly is are the times when he effortlessly does the thing he needs to do. He doesn’t think about it; he just does it. He’s evolving toward trying to make more effective decisions. Why do something based on what you think is right or wrong within a broken system when you can instead just try to change the thing that’s broken?

MH: It does seem like often he’s either doing the right thing for the wrong reasons or the wrong thing for the right reasons. He wants to be good, but his morality is all mixed up by the broken world he’s in.

AM: A little bit, yes. I find that to be true of so many young men. And I do think that some young men can get so lost. That’s what I’m always rooting for with Maximus—that he doesn’t get lost.

“I do think that SOME YOUNG MEN CAN GET SO LOST. That’s WHAT I’M ALWAYS ROOTING FOR with Maximus—that HE DOESN’T GET LOST.”

MH: The Brotherhood really exploits that. They seem to manipulate men who feel lost or insecure by using the language of knighthood and chivalric codes, these really old terms and power structures that are designed to impart upon men a sense of “masculine” purpose.

AM: I totally agree. Those words have power in them, and there’s control in that. There’s something extremely masculine about being a knight. It’s attractive to someone who feels like they have absolutely nothing to fight for.

MH: Then we see the Elder Cleric give up the game and show how those hierarchies betray men like Maximus. He’s made to feel like a hero, like a man of value to the Brotherhood, a rising star. Then the Elder Cleric treats him with such open contempt. How did you approach the scene where that fissure finally becomes apparent?

AM: One of the first things that actually made me connect with Maximus was his desire to find his people. If the world were to totally go to shit, the first thing I’d do would be to find my people. Friendship and family mean absolutely everything to me, and I think an experience is nothing when it’s not shared. Maximus so desperately wants to find his people that he lets those people hurt him. It really must be so painful to be fighting for your life—in an unfair fight—and to not have that hand reach out to help you. I love working with Michael Cristofer [who plays the Elder Cleric] because he’s one of those actors who, no matter how many times I read the script before I show up to set, will always find a way to read the scene in a way I wasn’t expecting. And he’ll hurt me more than I ever thought he could!

MH: There’s this pattern, almost a gag at this point, in which Maximus keeps unintentionally leaving a trail of bodies in his wake. He meets someone, he teams up with them, something goes wrong, they die. That happened again with Kumail Nanjiani’s character this season. What does that say about his journey?

AM: Well, one, it’s just entertaining, and two, it contributes to the sense of fragility we wanted to be ever present in the world of the show. There’s this cycle with Maximus where he’s been given all this power, especially with the power armor, but it comes with this weird irony. He’s constantly put into situations where he just wants someone to stop the fight, but they won’t. So he ends up in a position where the person he’s up against will do something horrible if Maximus doesn’t do something horrible first. That’s why we see Maximus have a growing connection to Lucy. He’s asking himself: What is he fighting through all of this to get to?

MH: You’ve said you deliberately haven’t played the Fallout games. I haven’t played myself, but I’m a gamer and I can’t resist going down rabbit holes online that explain the lore of games I haven’t played. The Fallout series has so much world building and lore—have you been tempted to learn more?

AM: That stuff is actually exciting to me too. So I do dive into the lore, mostly on YouTube and a little bit on Twitch, to hear people explain the different vault experiments and things we haven’t even touched on in the show yet. There are so many more creatures, factions, and elements of the Brotherhood that we haven’t gotten to.

MH: Do you have a favorite vault?

AM: One of the funniest ones I’ve seen was the vault filled entirely with clones of the same guy. That as a concept is hilarious to me. But it would also terrify me in real life. That would be a vault where I would immediately get back into the elevator and get out of there.

MH: I love the perverse humor of some of the vaults.

AM: Like some guy before the bombs dropped just thought, Why don’t we do one like this and see, 200-plus years later, what’s going on in there? It’s twisted. It’s so ridiculous.

MH: The tone of the games really gives the show permission to go to some outlandish places.

AM: And here we are at the end of season 2, and it’s only 16 hours into our telling. But the games are the kinds of games where people can spend 200 or 300 hours invested in the world. So there’s so much more that we’re going to tell. There are already all these secrets that the team has been burying in the show from the beginning. It’s going to be really great to see where this thing goes.
