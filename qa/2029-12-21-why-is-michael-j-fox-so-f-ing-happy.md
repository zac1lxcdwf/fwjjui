# Why Is Michael J. Fox So F#*!ing Happy?

Gus—big old Gus, a mutt made mostly of Great Dane, black-and-white, a savior of men, breathing deep, sleepy, dog-breath snorts—doesn’t always sleep in the master bedroom, but last night he did, and when Michael J. Fox cracked his eyelids open, the first thing he saw was Gus.

This made Fox happy. He can be relentlessly happy, and that can be hard for people to understand, given . . . everything. But yes, he woke up feeling pretty good.

Fox was on the floor because a lot of nights a bed just doesn’t work. He’s nearly 30 years into Parkinson’s disease, and the dyskinesias from his medications often cause him to tug and pull at the sheets until they are a sweaty tangle, which can’t be pleasant for Tracy, sleeping softly next to him. Plus, the mattress feels too forgiving against his writhing back. It’s better when the motion of his body moves against an immovable force, like the hard floor of his apartment, high above Central Park.

Sometimes, if he wakes up and the storms in his limbs and torso are quiet, he can fall back asleep. Today, though, he rose.

He shuffled down the short hall to the kitchen. Two years ago, in this kitchen, around this time of the morning, 6:30, he fell. He was home by himself—unusual for him, especially then, just four months into his recovery from a surgery to remove a tumor from his spine that could have paralyzed him. (The surgery, too, could have left him paralyzed. It didn’t.) He had landed hard on his left arm, shattering the bone, leaving him frozen in pain on the cold tile floor, struggling to reach his phone in the back pocket of his sweatshorts. Normally there are people around him to help—Tracy, of course, or one or more of their four children, who range in age from 19 to 31. His physical therapist, maybe. And Nina, his former assistant and now manager, seems never to be far.

But in that moment, he was something he hadn’t been for a long time: alone. In the 1980s, he buried himself in work and surrounded himself with an entourage of friends who liked to party and women who liked to date Michael J. Fox. And for years after his diagnosis in 1991, he walked in a kind of exoskeleton of his own design, built of optimism and humor, of love and high spirits—Parkinson’s disease was a gift, he had said. That shell now lay in invisible pieces all over the kitchen floor.

He had worked hard for decades to prop himself up with positivity and hope. But, he says, “I found myself sitting on that floor going, ‘This is fucked-up. I can’t put a happy face on this.’ And what did that mean that I couldn’t put a happy face on it? Does that mean it’s been bullshit all along, when I’ve tried to look at the positives?”

That was 2018. Today, a bright fall New York morning, was so far a much better day than that one.

He was walking well, for one thing—still sort of lurching sometimes, but no falls. He fixed himself the same breakfast he eats every morning: yogurt with bran and blueberries. The next and last stop of his early ritual was the seat in front of the television, where he turned on the show that has captured his attention during the quarantine: Alone, on which “they put people out in the middle of nowhere and you just watch them rot,” he says, smiling.

If Tracy were to poke her head in and find him watching it again, she would probably make that clicking sound she does, indicating mild disgust. (To which Fox would probably retort that she watches The Real Housewives of New York City, so there.) But Alone is actually good, he thinks. There are no camera crews—the show’s would-be survivors (Fox has also seen every episode of Survivor) film themselves and serve as their own narrators. “Something about it appealed to me while I was writing the book, because a lot of them are really eloquent with these tapes they make,” Fox says. “They set up cameras and talk into them on a daily basis and explain their experience. Some of them are really eloquent and articulate.”

How did he get to grateful and amazing?

HIS OFFICE is on the ground floor of his apartment building, and there are always ten things to do. Some business for the Michael J. Fox Foundation for Parkinson’s Research. Something for the new book. Yesterday he had to approve his likeness for a Marty McFly doll to be sold in Japan.

He stands now in the front room, which he calls his cave. He is five foot five and wears a black T-shirt, jeans with some slits worn through across the thighs. Sneakers (with laces, which he can tie just fine).

I get it out of the way that I grew up watching him. I skateboarded off the back of my brother’s pickup truck like Marty in Back to the Future, and I pushed up the sleeves of my Sunday blazer like Alex P. Keaton on Family Ties.

“If I’d known that, I wouldn’t have made you face the bragging wall,” he says, motioning to the shelves behind him, which hold his five Emmys and four Golden Globes, along with framed photos of him with a lot of cool people. There’s him and Springsteen at the Stone Pony, the night Fox played “Light of Day” up onstage with him—the song Bruce wrote for the 1987 movie of that name, starring Fox and Joan Jett as siblings in a garage band. Hidden gem of a movie.

Gus is here with him, half asleep on an enormous dog bed.

Fox, who is 59, spent all last week recording the audio version of No Time Like the Future in a small room down the hall, reading off an iPad strapped to a music stand fitted with a swatch of white carpet, for noise reduction. Taped to the wall in that room are bright-pink index cards delineating the themes of the book. The top card says themes, and the others beneath it read control, choice, prognosis, fear, mortality.

In his office for our interview, he chooses a rigid chair, which forces him to sit up. He’s thinking about the book. It can be a profound experience, reading your own words aloud for many hours a day, redoing certain sentences because there was too much saliva in your mouth or because a word caught in your throat and you had to wait for the emotion to pass. (That happened on page 131, when he read the part about Tracy giving a toast to him at a small dinner celebrating his 57th birthday. “I love you,” she had said, “and I believe in you,” and as he spoke her words in the small room down the hall, 30 years of Tracy’s devotion came crashing over him, and he had to stop for a moment.)

Within two minutes of sitting down, we’re talking about the nature of optimism and existence.

I ask him, How are you so upbeat? You’ve said that if you had the option of going back in time and not getting Parkinson’s, you wouldn’t change a thing. That, I confess, is hard to wrap one’s head around.

He nods a little at this.

“There’s the stuff you plan—the stuff you work towards, the stuff you aspire to, your desires and wishes and hopes,” he says. “And then there’s things that just happen. And the things that just happen are usually of a more intricate design and a higher purpose than whatever you come up with.”

He has timed his medications for this conversation, and his body is mostly still. Once in a while, his right hand bounces up and down on his thigh, then settles, like a leaf falling. There’s a light stubble on his chin, because shaving can be a tremulous adventure, so he often just skips it. He has the same good head of hair. The one eyebrow still cocks with mischief, and his blue eyes still dance.

“I had never in my life been really challenged,” he continues. “I mean, I was a kid and moved to L. A.”—in 1979, at age 18, he dropped out of high school and moved from Vancouver to Hollywood, with few prospects—“and ate shit for two years and tried to get jobs, and people turned me down because I was too short and funny looking and I had a funny accent. But then it worked out.”

That seemed hard at the time, he says. But Parkinson’s?

“There was no charming it.”

By the time he was diagnosed, he was drinking like—well, like a movie star in his 20s who had a ton of friends. “I mean, I was a semipopular kid growing up,” he says. “But then when everybody likes you? And every girl? And just, like, anything.” His timbre, which so far has been the low rasp of a guy about to turn 60, goes high and squeaky, like the freaked-out high school kid he played in Teen Wolf 35 years ago.

Fox had grown up a military kid, moving around a lot. His father did encryption and decoding work for the Canadian army; he died in 1990. His mother was a payroll clerk. I asked him what the ’80s were like for him. He shows me a picture on his phone of him with Rob Lowe and Judd Nelson, cracking up in the back of a bus. “It was so alien to what I grew up with, I didn’t have any defense mechanisms. I didn’t know to say, ‘Ah, you don’t have to bring those 20 people over to my hotel, or be doing shit that I don’t want people doing in my house.’ ”

Fox craved stability, and he found it in Tracy Pollan. Pollan had played Alex Keaton’s girlfriend Ellen on Family Ties. The two actors again found themselves working together in the 1988 film Bright Lights, Big City—in which Pollan played the stable, calming girlfriend to a young guy partying out of control.

“That saved me for a while. I started to calm down and be a little more mellow,” he says.

They married. Pollan gave birth to their first child, Sam, in 1989. Two years later, the Parkinson’s diagnosis came. “I just spun out again,” he says. “I started to drink heavily, and it was screwing up my relationships and screwing up my marriage and screwing up my work.” One morning, while filming the movie The Hard Way in New York City, he woke up on the couch in their Manhattan apartment. A bottle of warm Coors had spilled on the carpet. The weekday sun was streaming in through the windows, and he felt like he was sweating beer. Sam toddled around him, poking him.

Tracy walked in, on her way to the theater where she was performing in a play. She looked down at him. Not angry. Not even disappointed—they were past that.

“I was overwhelmed, with a little tiny kid and doing a play,” Pollan says. “And I remember thinking, Yeah, you know what? I think I’m done. This is when things change, or we move to a different place, because this isn’t going to work. I think he looked in my eyes and he realized, She’s not threatening me. She’s not even angry at me. She’s just sad.”

That half a Coors from the night before was the last drink he ever had.

“The tools that worked for quitting drinking work even better for this, which are: acceptance and surrender,” Fox says. “Not like, ‘I give up, I quit,’ but you just say, ‘Okay, I cede you the big points.’ With Parkinson’s, I’d reached a détente with it. An understanding. It was like, ‘You can take up this space, just leave me this space. And as it continues to shrink this space, I’ll find better ways to use it.’ And I was fine with that.”

His hand starts moving.

“And then this spine thing happened, and it just threw it all off. I didn’t count on that.”

Gus is suddenly breathing heavily, the symptom of an anxiety issue. Fox rises from his chair, and when he does, the effect is like an optical illusion: He is standing, and yet he is leaning, like a man in a windstorm. But he doesn’t fall.

He walks over to his dog and collapses gently to his knees. He cradles Gus’s head in his hands, comforting him.

“What’s up, Gus?” he says softly. “What’s up? You okay?” With Fox’s hands on his head, Gus slowly starts to breathe easier.

There’s a great line in Back to the Future—half a line, really—that came from Fox’s head. It’s a famous scene in a movie full of famous scenes: Marty McFly, Fox’s character, has just traveled back in time to 1955 and finds himself in the bedroom of his future mother, played by Lea Thompson, who’s still in high school. He’s resting in her bed after hitting his head, wearing only his Calvin Klein underwear.

“Michael is a genius idea guy, and he came over to me after we were laying the scene out,” says director Robert Zemeckis, who also cowrote the screenplay. “After he says, ‘Where are my pants?’ what was scripted is that Lea says, ‘Over there.’ Michael came over and said, ‘How about if she says, “Over there—on my hope chest.” ’ And I thought, Oh, shit, that’s hilarious. And Lea delivers the line perfectly. It was to his credit as a generous actor. That’s my favorite of all the lines he contributed.”

He is lights-out smart. You hear that a lot when you talk to anyone who knows Fox. Intelligent. Witty, which is the smart version of “funny.” And so quick.

“We share this common thing of barely going to high school and yet being successful, and that’s a strange position to be in,” says Thompson. “He is so smart and so facile and so generous. And so up to hit the ball back to you. I don’t really watch my movies that much, but I watched Back to the Future a few years ago, and what struck me was the kind of old-fashioned, classic comedy that he had, where it almost felt like he had bits. He had Laurel and Hardy. He would fall down, he would spit-take, he would double-take. The voice cracking. He had all of that in his arsenal. It was a perfect performance.”

His comedy took a slight turn in what he calls his second act: The roles he’s played in recent years, following his departure from Spin City in 2000, have been characters who also have Parkinson’s or something like it. Rescue Me. The fantastic, under-appreciated The Michael J. Fox Show. (Prime Video it.) A memorable turn on Curb Your Enthusiasm. And his biggest second-act role, the smarmy lawyer Louis Canning on The Good Wife.

Julianna Margulies, the star of the show, whose character was the frequent adversary of Canning in court, says it was “one of the biggest gifts of my life” when Fox joined the series. “There were moments when he would say something, I would say something, and then we would both literally be—I’m not saying this romantically—all into each other’s eyes,” she says. “The lock was so severe that all of a sudden we’d realize that neither of us knew our next lines. And then usually Robert and Michelle King”—the show’s creators—“would say, ‘Okay, that’s all we need. We don’t need the lines that come after that.’ ”

And Fox was funny as all hell.

The little rooms in hospitals where the doctor tells you what’s happening are always well lit. There’s usually an exam bed with a crisp sheet of white paper pulled over it, and not enough chairs. A blood-pressure machine is affixed to the wall; cords and devices and tubes hang silently. In these rooms, there is no world outside, only you inside a small box painted in neutral colors.

Or sometimes it’s in an office—the medical books, the diploma on the wall, the family photo on the desk.

Mike Fox and Tracy Pollan have sat together in more of these rooms than either can remember. Fox listens, some of the words floating past him as he tries to comprehend what’s being said about the circuitry and misfires going on in his brain and his body.

Tracy, in these moments, holds a pen tight and writes on the spiral notebook in her lap. Someone has to write it down. Otherwise you find yourself trying to remember later that night what the doctor said. Are we definitely upping the dosage next week, or does he want to see you again first? And one of them would probably make a joke. (“Laughter’s a defense mechanism,” he says. “It’s like showing your teeth to whatever threatens you. It’s like saying, ‘Ha ha, I don’t give a shit!’ It’s the first place we go.”)

It’s hard to explain, but that simple act of taking the notes is somehow enormous.

They got married in 1988. By the time of his diagnosis about three years later, he had something that made him strong before he ever knew how strong he would have to be: He had Tracy.

“I can normalize everything. I can make like it’s all normal, like it just is what it is,” Fox says. His hand is tremoring, and he doesn’t attempt to calm it. “And then sometimes I get moments when I think, This isn’t normal. This sucks. But then to have someone say, ‘That’s normal. And I’m okay with that.’ Oh, okay—she says it’s normal, and she’s okay with it. So let me rethink it. It’s kinda like if you’re walking along with someone and you’re wearing a silly hat, and you look over and they’ve got a silly hat on, too, you go, ‘Ah! Regardless of what anyone else thinks, we both think this silly-hat thing works.’ Just to have a comrade. It’s so much more complicated than ‘It’s a great marriage.’ It’s a great understanding. It depends on mutual goodwill and mutual love and mutual concern. I mean, the nightmare is, you look over to the other person when you’re dealing with this tragedy, and they’re not there. They’re on a phone call somewhere. But if you look over and—they may not have a smile on their face, but they’re there. It means everything in the world.”

The walk from the front door of his apartment building around the corner to his office takes about a minute and a half.

He holds a cane in his right hand. His left leg swivels wide with each step, setting down in front of him at the last second each time—there is a janky rhythm to it, and he’s got it figured out.

Even during these pandemic days, we all see people all the time, and we have no idea what’s behind their eyes, or what they’ve left at home. We all put up a front.

For a long time, even as his condition deteriorated, Fox went about his days, appearing fine. The optimism he wrote about in his books, and told People magazine about, and projected at events for the foundation—that was real. He did feel confident. But he did not always feel grateful, and he did not always feel amazing. And so he worked at it—in Jungian analysis, through transcendental meditation, with his wife, and by the singular willpower of his own mind. He built a scaffolding of hope and positivity and deployed that smile he somehow never lost, even when it didn’t come easy.

And now, as he walks down the leafy street to his cave, on a sunny autumn day in New York, unselfconscious about his gait, his dog loping along behind him?

Now the smile comes easy.

“For a long time, he was working on this optimistic mind-set and driving toward that,” says his son, Sam, 31. “But once you can stop working on it and have it just be your life, that’s when you can be happy about a moment: when you don’t have to sit there and go, ‘Okay, this thing happened to me, how am I gonna play it? How am I going to make this something that I can be optimistic about?’ Once you don’t have to think about that, you can just be happy about your circumstance.”

They still happen, those moments he has to fight through. “I had one yesterday,” Fox says. He was on the floor doing some stretching—his knee constantly tries to hyperextend, to the point where his tendons are chronically sore, and he does regular stretching to keep it in place.

His phone was in his hand, and he was trying to get up off the floor. This is a complicated process, the fascia of his compact body loosening and tightening in such a way that he can become upright, or his version of upright. It hurt. He went to put the phone on a table—the one that holds his medicines and framed photos of the kids and last year’s Valentine’s Day card from Tracy—and the phone bounced onto the floor.

“And I said, ‘Fuck this.’ ”

But here is what’s different now: “It passes. It’s just a little moment,” he says. “They’re not big moments like, Why me? It’s like, That sucks. That hurt. Or like, I’m trying to push the right button on the phone and I call Zimbabwe. Or I’m trying to find ‘pet milk’ and I get ‘wet MILFs,’ so there’s porn coming out of my phone. But I just, um—it’s what it is.”

Tracy points to an even larger view. “If you wish certain things in your life away,” she says, “you don’t know what else you’re wishing away. If this hadn’t happened, so many other things might not have happened. We might not have all of the children that we have, or we may have—it’s just so hard to wish any part of it away. Even the hard things, because we learn so much from the harder things.”

He has built a life from the ruins of his life.

Inside his office, there is some work to be done. He has physical therapy at four, on Zoom—his Allbirds, socks, and shorts are on the shelf under the TV. But at the moment, he calls Gus over. Big old Gus. Fox stands from his chair, then begins the long, unsteady process of lowering himself to the floor, to be next to his dog for a few quiet minutes.

Fox props himself on one elbow, and once he is all the way down, his body is calm, its motion meeting the immovable force of the floor. Gus licks his face, and this makes Fox happy. He can be relentlessly happy. And you can understand that.
