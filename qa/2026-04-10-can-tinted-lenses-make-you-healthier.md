IN CASE you haven’t heard, we’re not just wearing blue blockers before bed anymore. Now, everyone’s wearing tinted lenses, all the time, all in the name of good health.

Biohacker-in-Chief Andrew Huberman is collaborating with ROKA on a line. Dave Asprey’s brand TrueDark offers “true 24-hour light management,” with amber tints for daytime and red for evening. WHOOP sells swaggy evening and “all-day” lenses. Powerhouses from Norwegian soccer star Erling Haaland to mega-producer Rick Rubin have them on in photo after photo.

The people who swear by (and sell) these shades claim they’ll boost your wellbeing across the board: Better sleep, energy, mood, stress levels, and performance. Asprey’s company claims their lenses let you “work smarter, sleep deeper, and feel your best.” Here’s what we found out about where the trend’s benefits are and aren’t.

What the Science Says about Tinted Lenses

TOO MUCH light exposure at night can trick your brain and body into thinking it’s daytime. So getting less blue light in the evening can help support your circadian rhythm (your body’s internal clock), the release of melatonin, and your sleep cycle, says Raj Maturi, M.D., a spokesperson for the American Academy of Ophthalmology (AAO) and ophthalmologist at the Midwest Eye Institute in Indianapolis.

But most experts, including Maturi and the AAO, recommend just dimming artificial lights and turning on night mode on your screens or using light-filtering apps two to three hours before bed. It won’t look as rockstar, but it also won’t set you back 100 bucks. The research on blue-light glasses is pretty flimsy, says Andrew W. McHill, Ph.D, an assistant professor in the Sleep, Chronobiology, and Health Laboratory at Oregon Health & Science University. Yet McHill will go as far as saying that they can probably get the job done in the evening if you enjoy wearing them, but it’s not like they’re a must.

But when it comes to wearing tinted glasses 24/7, experts are more skeptical. For most people, “It's actually detrimental, probably,” McHill says. Some of the companies selling them even specify that the evening versions aren’t to be used round the clock. Getting lots of daytime light—whether it’s natural or artificial—actually helps keep our brains awake, suppresses melatonin, and synchronizes our internal clocks, McHill says. Natural light in the day is generally good for everything that tinted lens devotees claim their glasses help with, he adds: sleep, mood, energy, stress. (And maybe a whole lot more: One study on over 88,000 people found a link between higher daytime light exposure and a lower risk of dying from any cause.)

OK, but is there such thing as too much daytime artificial light? Yes and no. There’s no evidence that blue light exposure during the day is harmful, Maturi says—whether it comes from the sky (the biggest source of blue light, BTW) or a screen. In fact, some research suggests that blue light exposure might also improve cognitive performance and alertness, says Muriel Schornack, O.D., an optometrist at the Mayo Clinic.

But it is known that staring at a laptop or scrolling TikTok for six hours can cause eye strain and discomfort due to a combination of light exposure, focusing, and squinting, McHill says. “Too much of one thing can always be bad.”

So for extended screen time, Schornack says, people might find blue-light filtering glasses helpful, but be aware that the research still isn’t there on this. (Also: “Remember to blink,” Maturi says, which lubricates your eyes. And to keep discomfort away, take breaks from the screen. The AAO recommends the 20-20-20 rule: Every 20 minutes, look at an object 20 feet away for 20 seconds.)

It's also possible that not everyone responds the same way to tinted lenses. There is some interesting evidence that certain individuals stand to benefit from tinted lenses more than others, Schornack says. Some people are just naturally more light-sensitive, McHill says, especially to blue or fluorescent light. For them, wearing tinted glasses to help soften more intense sources of artificial light exposure during the day (like a computer screen or fluorescently lit office) may keep their eyes more comfortable.

The same goes for people with conditions that can cause light sensitivity, like dry eye, traumatic brain injuries (TBI), and migraines. Research shows that a type of rose-tinted glasses, called FL-41 lenses, that block specific wavelengths of blue-green and fluorescent light can improve symptoms in people with chronically dry eyes, eye pain, and light sensitivity. Some evidence also suggests that various colors of tinted lenses can relieve light sensitivity in people with TBI.

And most people who get migraines experience heightened sensitivity to light—even when they’re not having an attack, according to the American Migraine Foundation. Schornack points to one small study that showed tinted lenses could help reduce eye discomfort, as well as hyperactivity in the visual center of the brain, in migraine sufferers.

Where to Find Tinted Lenses

Should you get tinted lenses?

IF YOU'RE dealing with a condition that’s making you sensitive to light, talk to your doc about whether certain shades (or other treatments) could help. But most people don’t need tinted glasses. And wearing them could make you miss out on the benefits of daytime light for your brain’s wakefulness and circadian rhythm, McHill says.

If you’re really feeling the trend, though, just fight the urge to make it a 24/7 thing. And consider this as you’re choosing the color:

Go Rose If…

…you’re dealing with dry eye, light sensitivity, TBI, or migraines. Try rose-tinted FL-41 lenses, since they have some studies behind them.

Go Orange If…

…blue-blocking at night is what you’re after, and you want more than light-filtering apps and dimming the lights can give you. If tinted lenses do have an effect, “from the literature, it appears that orange-tinted may be the most effective,” McHill says. One study found that even super-cheap orange-tinted lenses are as effective at filtering out short wavelengths of light as pricier blue blockers.
